\abstract{%
Work on group robustness is measured almost entirely by worst-group accuracy, and it is
natural to assume that a model made accurate on its worst group will also give that
group reliable prediction sets---sets that contain the right label as often as they
promise.
We find that the two come apart. Across two spurious-correlation benchmarks and four
backbones, worst-group coverage depends far more on how the scores are turned into sets
than on how the model was trained: under one threshold shared by all groups, an ordinary
model covers $89\%$ of all test images but only $51\%$ of those in its worst group;
give each
group its own threshold and most of that gap closes under every training
method we tried, on the same model and the same scores. Fine-tuning the representation
end-to-end does not change this. What training does buy is smaller sets for the worst
group, and only once calibration is group-conditional. If the worst group has to be
reliable, the calibration rule is the place to start.
}
\keywords{conformal prediction, spurious correlation, group robustness, worst-group coverage, group-conditional calibration}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A waterbird standing on land is still a waterbird. A classifier trained on images
where waterbirds are nearly always over water will often disagree. Conformal prediction
\citep{vovk2005,angelopoulos2020,romano2020} is meant to keep such a model honest about
what it does not know: instead of committing to one label it returns a set, calibrated
so the true label falls inside it with a stated probability. Across the whole test set
it does what it promises. In one of the settings we study, the sets contain the right
species $89\%$ of the time against a $90\%$ target. For the birds on the wrong
background, the same sets contain it $51\%$ of the time.

The guarantee was never violated. It was marginal, and a marginal promise says nothing
about any particular group \citep{cresswell2024}. Two lines of work address the failure
underneath it and rarely meet. One changes how the model is trained, so that the worst
group becomes accurate. The other changes how the scores are calibrated, so that
coverage holds group by group. They meet at an assumption that is widely held and rarely tested: that a representation
made accurate on its worst group will also make that group conformally reliable, so
robust training should pay off for the uncertainty estimates built on top of it.

It does not. Holding the model fixed and changing only the calibration rule lifts worst-group
coverage by up to $0.345$, and once every group has its own threshold the training method stops
mattering, while under one shared threshold it decides the outcome. The effect survives
fine-tuning the representation end-to-end. The size of the effect has a measurable source: the shortfall a shared
threshold produces is bounded by the Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance between the worst group's score
distribution and everyone else's. Across our eight settings that distance also tracks how much a
per-group threshold recovers, an association rather than a prediction.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The calibration rule moves worst-group coverage far more than the training
method does (Section~\ref{sec:c1}).} Across eight backbone--dataset settings, coverage spreads
across training methods by up to $0.358$ under one shared threshold and by at most $0.024$ under
per-group thresholds, both at APS. Fine-tuning the backbone end-to-end does not change this
(Section~\ref{sec:repr}).

\item \textbf{The size of that effect tracks the score geometry
(Section~\ref{sec:c1}).} The shortfall under a shared threshold obeys the Kolmogorov--Smirnov bound
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ksbound} in every setting, tightly on Waterbirds and loosely on CelebA. This is a
check of Proposition~\ref{prop:dissociation} on our data, not a new result.

\item \textbf{What training does buy is smaller sets, and only once calibration is
group-conditional (Section~\ref{sec:c2}).} With coverage matched across methods, the worst group's
set size still differs between them by $0.022$ to $0.259$. The best robust method gives smaller
sets than ERM in five of the six settings where ERM is retained, but every per-setting correlation
is uninformative once an interval is attached, so we report a tendency, not a ranking rule.

\item \textbf{A matched-accuracy protocol, and how little survives it
(Section~\ref{sec:h1}).} Comparing cross-group divergence at matched model-level accuracy leaves
one of eight settings with overlapping support, because robust training moves base accuracy away
from ERM's. Uncontrolled comparisons therefore credit training with a reduction that cannot be
separated from an accuracy effect.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Group-robust training.} Worst-group accuracy is improved by
distributionally robust optimization \citep{sagawa2020}, last-layer retraining on
group-balanced data (DFR) \citep{kirichenko2023}, automatic feature reweighting
(AFR) \citep{qiu2023}, and pseudo-group methods such as JTT \citep{liu2021jtt} and
CNC \citep{zhang2022cnc}. The standard subpopulation-shift benchmark
\citep{yang2023change} compares these on accuracy-family metrics and calibration
error but reports no set-valued or coverage quantities. We take our last-layer methods from this literature, so the interventions we study are the ones
it reports, and ask what they do to the \emph{conformal} side, which this literature does not
measure. Selective classification under group
shift \citep{jones2021} found that abstention can leave worst-group accuracy flat; our set-valued
analysis extends that line to conformal prediction.

\paragraph{Group-conditional and fair conformal prediction.} Mondrian conformal prediction \citep{vovk2003mondrian,vovk2005} computes
group-conditional thresholds; conditional
guarantees beyond disjoint groups are given by \citet{gibbs2023} and
\citet{jung2023}, and \citet{deng2025kandinsky} extend group-conditional coverage
to overlapping and fractional memberships. On fairness, \citet{cresswell2024} show
that equalizing coverage can increase disparate impact and recommend equalizing
set sizes; \citet{afcp2024} adaptively equalize coverage while controlling
efficiency. Equalized coverage as a fairness standard for prediction sets is due to
\citet{romano2020equalized}, and \citet{burden2026} prove that it and equalized set
size cannot in general hold simultaneously, recasting the calibration policy as a
choice of \emph{where} cross-group heterogeneity manifests. Our work is the
empirical complement on the axis these papers hold fixed. We vary the
\emph{training} intervention, and with it the representation and the score distribution, and ask
whether the conserved heterogeneity shrinks at the source. It does not, and the
calibration policy remains decisive.

\paragraph{Conformal prediction under shift.}
\citet{tibshirani2019} establish validity under covariate shift via likelihood
reweighting; multi-distribution robust conformal prediction \citep{mdcp2026}
targets uniform worst-case coverage as a group-DRO analogue \emph{inside the
calibration procedure}, holding the model fixed---consistent with our finding that
the calibration mechanism is the lever that matters. No prior work crosses the
training interventions with calibration rules under a correlation-strength
sweep, nor controls for the accuracy confound we isolate.



\paragraph{Group recovery and conditional coverage.} Biased ERM representations encode the
spurious attribute strongly, the principle behind JTT and CNC, and our probe confirms it in every
setting (Section~\ref{sec:predgroup}). Conformal prediction under noisy class or group labels
\citep{einbinder2022,sesia2023noisy} corrects coverage but assumes the group is given; here the
group is \emph{recoverable}, which removes the auditability barrier and, through predicted groups,
the deployment barrier. Exact feature-conditional coverage is impossible distribution-free in finite
samples \citep{lei2014distribution,barber2021limits}, so group-conditional coverage is the practical
target. We hold that calibration family fixed and vary the training intervention instead.

\section{Problem Setup and Notation}
\label{sec:setup}

This section fixes notation, states the result that separates the two calibration rules, and
defines the matched-accuracy comparison. We observe triples $(x,y,a)$ with image $x$, label $y\in\mathcal{Y}$, and binary
spurious attribute $a\in\{0,1\}$ (background/\emph{place} for Waterbirds;
\emph{Male} for CelebA). Groups are $g=(y,a)\in\mathcal{G}$,
$|\mathcal{G}|=2|\mathcal{Y}|$. A frozen backbone $\phi$ maps $x$ to features
$\phi(x)\in\mathbb{R}^d$; a last-layer head $f_\theta$ trained by intervention $m$
maps $\phi(x)$ to class scores. The \emph{correlation strength} $\rho$ is the
evaluation probability that $a=y$; we
calibrate at $\rhocal=0.95$ and evaluate over
$\rhotest\in\{0.95,0.9,0.8,0.7,0.6,0.5\}$, holding the calibration distribution
fixed.

\paragraph{Conformal sets and calibration policies.} Throughout, ``calibration''
means the conformal step that turns scores into a threshold, not the probability
calibration measured by expected calibration error \citep{guo2017calibration}. Given a nonconformity score
$s(x,y)$ and a calibration set $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$, split conformal
prediction computes
$\hat q=\mathrm{Quantile}_{1-\alpha}\big(\{s(x_i,y_i)\}_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}}\big)$
and returns $C(x)=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}: s(x,y)\le\hat q\}$. We use three scores: THR
($s_{\mathrm{THR}}(x,y)=1-\hat p(y\mid x)$); APS \citep{romano2020}, the cumulative
softmax mass up to $y$; and RAPS \citep{angelopoulos2020}, APS with a tail
regularizer. We compare three \emph{calibration policies}: \textbf{marginal split}
(one global $\hat q$); \textbf{Mondrian} (a separate $\hat q_g$ per group, with a candidate label
$c$ admitted when $s(x,c)\le\hat q_{g(c)}$, the stratum that label would place the point in, so
that the set is computable without the test label); and
\textbf{shift-robust} (a worst-case threshold over a total-variation ball around the calibration
distribution, whose radius is the observed shift). Target $1-\alpha=0.90$ throughout; the full configuration is in
Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}.

\paragraph{Reliability quantities.} For group $g$, $\cov_g=\Pr[y\in C(x)\mid g]$
and $|C|_g$ is its mean set size. We report worst-group coverage $\min_g\cov_g$
and the \emph{coverage gap}, i.e.\ the worst group's shortfall from the target,
\begin{equation}
\covgap=(1-\alpha)-\min_g\cov_g,
\label{eq:covgap}
\end{equation}
so $\covgap>0$ exactly when the worst group is under-covered. It is the quantity tabulated
throughout. We also report mean set size, the set-size disparity
$\max_g|C|_g-\min_g|C|_g$, and the unweighted mean over groups
$|\mathcal{G}|^{-1}\sum_g\cov_g$, which is the level group-conditional calibration targets. The
mean matters because a minimum over a few groups is biased downward (Appendix~\ref{app:subtarget}).

\paragraph{Cross-group score divergence.} Following the
conserved-quantity view of \citet{burden2026}, we summarize cross-group score
heterogeneity by the divergence between the worst group's conformity-score
distribution $P_{\wg}$ and that of the remaining groups $P_{\neg\wg}$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
D &= \Wone(P_{\wg},P_{\neg\wg}) \quad\text{(Wasserstein-1)}, \\
D_{\mathrm{KS}} &= \sup_t\big|F_{\wg}(t)-F_{\neg\wg}(t)\big| \quad\text{(Kolmogorov--Smirnov)},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:div}
\end{equation}
computed per score on a held-out split.

\paragraph{A dissociation result.}

\begin{proposition}[Calibration--representation dissociation]
\label{prop:dissociation}
Fix a score $s$ and target $1-\alpha$, and assume within-group exchangeability of
calibration and test scores.
\emph{(i)} Group-conditional (Mondrian) split conformal, with per-group threshold
$\hat q_g$ the $\lceil(1-\alpha)(n_g+1)\rceil$-th smallest calibration score in
group $g$, satisfies $\Pr\!\big(Y\in C(X)\mid G=g\big)\ge 1-\alpha$ for every group
$g$, with no dependence on $s$ beyond exchangeability.
\emph{(ii)} Let the scores be continuous, let $\pi=\Pr(G=\wg)$ under the calibration
distribution, and let $F_g$ be the group-$g$ nonconformity-score CDF. Marginal split
conformal uses the single pooled threshold $\hat q$ solving
$\pi F_{\wg}(\hat q)+(1-\pi)F_{\neg\wg}(\hat q)=1-\alpha$, and has per-group coverage
$\Pr\!\big(Y\in C(X)\mid G=g\big)=F_g(\hat q)$. The worst-group shortfall then obeys
the exact identity
\begin{equation}
(1-\alpha)-F_{\wg}(\hat q)\;=\;(1-\pi)\,\big[F_{\neg\wg}(\hat q)-F_{\wg}(\hat q)\big],
\label{eq:shortfall}
\end{equation}
and consequently
\begin{equation}
(1-\alpha)-F_{\wg}(\hat q)\;\le\;(1-\pi)\,D_{\mathrm{KS}}.
\label{eq:ksbound}
\end{equation}
Hence the representation (through $s$, and through any training intervention that
changes $s$) controls worst-group coverage \emph{only} under marginal calibration;
under Mondrian it does not. \end{proposition}

\noindent The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. \citet{jones2021} decompose the margin
distribution the same way to ask when abstention helps the worst group, with a confidence cut in
place of a conformal quantile and accuracy in place of coverage; their negative answer and ours
share one source, a single threshold read against two different score distributions.

\noindent\textbf{Remark (what the divergences do and do not give).} It does not follow
from \eqref{eq:shortfall} that the shortfall grows with the divergence $D$ of
\eqref{eq:div}, and in general it does not. What is true: the shortfall is the
cross-group CDF gap \emph{at the threshold}, scaled by $(1-\pi)$, and $D_{\mathrm{KS}}$
bounds it because it is a supremum over $t$. $\Wone$ admits no such bound: the gap can
concentrate on a vanishing interval, sending $\Wone$ to zero while the shortfall stays
fixed (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}). We therefore use $\Wone$ only as a descriptive summary, its role in
\citet{burden2026}. Neither divergence orders coverage across models without assuming
the same group is worst in each, and we do not assume it: \eqref{eq:shortfall} and
\eqref{eq:ksbound} are applied evaluation by evaluation to whichever group is in fact worst. The
assumption is a good approximation for the ERM baseline under a shared threshold, where one group
is the worst in $85$--$100\%$ of evaluations, and a poor one under per-group thresholds, where no
group holds the minimum in more than $41\%$ of evaluations and the most concentrated setting is closer to the
$25\%$ of a four-way tie than to the shared-threshold figure (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}).

Part (i) holds by construction, so what we test is part (ii): how large the gap of
\eqref{eq:shortfall} is in practice, and whether training shrinks it beyond what its accuracy gain
explains (Sections~\ref{sec:c1} and~\ref{sec:h1}). The factor $(1-\pi)$ exposes a small group most.
That describes Waterbirds, where the worst group under a shared threshold holds $2.5\%$ of the
calibration set, but not CelebA, where compositing to $\rhocal=0.95$ leaves it $47.5\%$ and the
shortfall comes from the score geometry of \eqref{eq:shortfall}.

Proposition~\ref{prop:dissociation} is a population statement, and the empirical Mondrian level of
$0.83$--$0.89$ sits below it for two finite-sample reasons: we report a \emph{minimum} over
$|\mathcal{G}|$ noisy per-group coverages, biased low even when every group is valid, and each
Mondrian threshold rests on its own group's calibration sample, of which the minority groups hold a
few percent. Section~\ref{sec:c1} shows the mean over groups at the nominal level, and
Section~\ref{sec:h3} ties the shortfall to calibration-set size.

\paragraph{Group recoverability.} To test whether the deployed representation
hides the protected group, we measure the test $\auroc$ of a probe
$h:\phi(x)\mapsto a$ trained in-domain to recover the spurious attribute.

\paragraph{Divergence at matched accuracy.} $D$ in \eqref{eq:div} and $\covgap$ in
\eqref{eq:covgap} are entangled with base accuracy: a more accurate model produces
sharper, more group-aligned score distributions, so ``robust training lowers $D$''
partly restates ``robust training raises accuracy''. For each method $m$ and the ERM
reference we collect per-seed pairs $\big(\mathrm{acc}^{(s)}_m,D^{(s)}_m\big)$ and read
the divergence at a common accuracy $a^\star$ in the \emph{overlapping} accuracy
support:
\begin{equation}
\Delta(a^\star)=D_{\mathrm{ERM}}(a^\star)-D_m(a^\star),
\label{eq:matched}
\end{equation}
with a $95\%$ two-stage cluster-bootstrap CI, so $\Delta(a^\star)>0$ means $m$ lowers the
divergence at matched accuracy. Where the supports do not overlap,
$\Delta(a^\star)$ is \textbf{undefined} and reported as \texttt{unmatched}; the raw $D$
appears separately, labelled uncontrolled.

\paragraph{Runs and exclusions.} We call one (backbone, dataset, training method, seed) tuple a
\emph{run}, and one conformal evaluation of a run, at a given score, calibration split and
$\rhotest$, an \emph{evaluation}. Each run must reach the worst-group accuracy floor set for its
training method or it is excluded, with the reason recorded. Probes are standardised
(StandardScaler~$\to$~logistic regression), trained in-domain, and never see a test label, and
neither do marginal split and Mondrian. Shift-robust calibration does: it sizes its ball from test
scores at their true labels, so we report it as an upper bound, not a deployable rule.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp}

\paragraph{Datasets and backbones.} We use Waterbirds and CelebA (blond-hair
classification, with \emph{Male} as the spurious attribute) and four frozen backbones from two
architecture families and three pretraining regimes: an ERM-trained
ResNet-50 ($d{=}2048$, the canonical setup), CLIP ViT-B/32 ($d{=}512$, image--text
contrastive), DINOv2 ViT-B/14 ($d{=}768$, self-supervised and never label-trained), and
a supervised ImageNet ViT-B/16 ($d{=}768$). Features are cached once per backbone.
Section~\ref{sec:repr} additionally fine-tunes the ResNet-50 end-to-end under three
objectives, so the representation itself moves, not only the head. Both tasks are binary
($|\mathcal{Y}|=2$) with four groups $g=(y,a)$; cached split sizes are in
Table~\ref{tab:data} and exact per-group counts are released with the artifacts.

\paragraph{Correlation strength.} For a target $\rho$ we resample the evaluation rows
so that $a=y$ with probability $\rho$ and the two classes are equal in size; the realised $\rho$ is
logged per draw. Calibration uses $\rhocal=0.95$; final-test coverage is read at each
$\rhotest\in\{0.95,0.9,0.8,0.7,0.6,0.5\}$. The evaluation pools start near independence
($\rho\approx0.51$ on Waterbirds, $0.47$ on CelebA). On Waterbirds $a=y$ is the training
association, waterbirds over water; on CelebA, whose raw data pair blond hair with being female,
it is the reverse one.

\paragraph{Uncertainty.} Point estimates are means over five training seeds for the
main grid, five for the fine-tuning study on Waterbirds and three on CelebA, whose training set
is thirty-four times larger, and three for the
calibration comparison, each with ten calibration splits. The comparison uses fewer seeds because it crosses every
training method with three calibration rules, six correlation strengths and three
scores, and two of its five methods ignore the seed anyway. Intervals are two-stage cluster
bootstraps ($B=2000$), except the correlation intervals of Table~\ref{tab:c2}, which resample
methods; Table~\ref{tab:variance} reports the seed and split standard deviations separately.

\paragraph{Training methods.} On frozen features we fit
ERM, DFR, AFR, balanced subsampling \citep{idrissi2022balancing}, and last-layer
GroupDRO (minutes each on cached
features). Section~\ref{sec:repr} covers the full-network case separately.

\paragraph{Deterministic solvers.} ERM and AFR fit their heads with L-BFGS,
whose solver ignores the random seed, so they produce the same head every time: their
across-seed standard deviation is exactly $0.000$ and their base accuracy is identical
to five decimals in every setting. Extra seeds therefore add replicates for DFR,
balanced subsampling and GroupDRO-LL (across-seed SD up to $0.027$ in worst-group
accuracy) and none for ERM or AFR, whose SD is exactly zero in every setting. This is also why every interval here, apart from the
correlation intervals, is a cluster bootstrap that resamples training seeds and then calibration
splits within them: the ten splits inside a seed share one trained model.

\paragraph{Exclusions.} Of the $200$ runs, $33$ fall below the worst-group accuracy floor
set for their method and are excluded from the main analysis. They are AFR on all four CelebA
backbones (all seeds, $0.013$--$0.434$ against a floor of $0.75$), ERM on CelebA with the
self-supervised and supervised ViT features ($0.203$ and $0.263$ against $0.30$), and two
GroupDRO-LL seeds and one balanced-subsampling seed on CelebA/ResNet-50. Twelve DFR runs on
CelebA fall between DFR's floor of $0.80$ and its advisory reference of $0.85$; they are kept and flagged. We
fixed these floors and the six hypotheses of Appendix~\ref{app:hyps} before running the experiments
they concern, but did not lodge them in a public registry, so they are pre-specified rather than
preregistered. Appendix~\ref{app:excl} repeats the analysis with the excluded runs put back.
ERM's worst-group accuracy spans $0.20$--$0.84$ and falls below $0.30$ in two settings, a sign of
strong shortcut learning, while the robust methods average $0.63$--$0.95$; ERM reaches that range
only on DINOv2/Waterbirds, the Waterbirds setting where the shared threshold was closest to target.

\paragraph{Comparison with published values.} Table~\ref{tab:lit} places our
ResNet-50 methods beside published worst-group accuracies. The gaps are large, and the comparison
is not like-for-like for two reasons. We refit only the last layer on features from a deliberately
light recipe, whereas the published GroupDRO numbers come from end-to-end training and the published
DFR and AFR numbers retrain a last layer on a stronger base model; and we read accuracy on our pooled
evaluation domain. DFR comes closest ($0.851$ against $0.883$ on CelebA). AFR, which reweights the
base model's own errors, is furthest on CelebA, where it degenerates (Appendix~\ref{app:excl}); on
Waterbirds last-layer GroupDRO is furthest. The gaps
do not affect our conclusions, because every result here compares the same model and the same
posteriors calibrated two ways. A weaker backbone lowers all methods in a setting together, and the
four backbones, which span $0.20$--$0.95$ in worst-group accuracy, behave alike.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Five results follow. The calibration rule moves worst-group coverage further than the training
method does, retraining the representation end-to-end does not change that, and what training buys
instead is smaller sets. The last two test the training side on its own terms: the
divergence it is credited with reducing, measured at matched accuracy, and the deployment
conditions the result has to survive.

\subsection{Calibration Rule versus Training Method}
\label{sec:c1}

Table~\ref{tab:c1} reports worst-group coverage under both calibration rules for every
training method and setting. Under one shared threshold, coverage follows the training method and
spreads by up to $0.358$. With a threshold per group, the spread never exceeds $0.024$, and an
equivalence check keeps the upper end of its one-sided bootstrap interval below a $0.05$ margin in
all eight settings.
Every lift interval excludes zero. With three training seeds, these intervals give a direction and
a rough width, not exact coverage.

That margin was not pre-specified. We chose it after seeing the spreads, as the largest coverage
difference we would consider immaterial in deployment. It is also wide compared with the spreads
themselves, so the test bounds the effect but does not resolve it. At a margin of $0.04$ the result
would be seven of eight, since CLIP/Waterbirds reaches an upper bound of $0.044$ and
DINOv2/Waterbirds clears it by less than $0.001$.

The cleanest comparison is the same ERM model under the two rules
(Figure~\ref{fig:c1}). On CLIP/Waterbirds it moves from $0.509$ to $0.854$, matching
what DFR reaches, with nothing retrained. The gain is largest where the shared
threshold failed worst, and the two added backbones behave like the original two. The switch is not
free everywhere: in seven of the thirty-four retained (setting, method) pairs it costs coverage, by
at most $0.014$, always where the shared threshold was already near target.

The size of the gain can be anticipated before re-calibrating, by two results of different
strength. The first is the bound $(1-\pi)D_{\mathrm{KS}}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ksbound}.
Figure~\ref{fig:mech} (left) shows
that the bound holds in all eight settings, applied evaluation by evaluation to whichever group the
shared threshold leaves worst. It is not uniformly tight: the shortfall reaches $0.59$--$0.93$ of the bound
on Waterbirds but only $0.43$--$0.58$ on CelebA, so the bound is informative where the effect is
large.

The second is an association. $D_{\mathrm{KS}}$, measured under the shared threshold and pooled
over the kept runs, tracks the gain from per-group thresholds with $r=0.93$
(Figure~\ref{fig:mech}, right). That orders coverage by divergence across settings, which no
divergence licenses on its own (Section~\ref{sec:setup}), and the eight points fall into two dataset
clusters with no interval, so we read it as an observed association, not a prediction. Where the
two groups' score distributions nearly coincide, as on CelebA with DINOv2 and ViT-B/16, there is
little to recover: the effect follows the score geometry, which is measurable in advance, not the
choice of backbone.

\subsection{Retraining the Representation}
\label{sec:repr}

Every result so far holds the backbone fixed and varies the last layer. On its own that
cannot separate ``calibration beats last-layer adaptation'' from ``calibration beats robust
training in general''. Here we fine-tune the backbone \emph{end-to-end} under three objectives
(plain ERM, GroupDRO and group reweighting), with five fine-tuning seeds each on Waterbirds and
three on CelebA, and repeat the comparison on the resulting representations. The head is held fixed at plain ERM, so the
representation is the only thing that moves. How much of a pretrained
network to unfreeze changes what the resulting representation encodes, and full
fine-tuning can distort pretrained features relative to linear probing
\citep{kumar2022finetuning,elmehdi2024unfreezing}. We unfreeze all of it, the setting in
which the representation moves most, which pulls it as far as each objective
allows and gives calibration the hardest test our setting can offer.

\paragraph{Manipulation check.} A representation-level experiment is informative only
if the intervention actually moved the representation, so we verify that first.
GroupDRO fine-tuning raises worst-group accuracy from $0.516$ to $0.738$ on
Waterbirds ($+0.222$) and from $0.396$ to $0.470$ on CelebA ($+0.074$), the largest
gain any of our representation-level objectives produces. It is not the largest in the paper---DFR
moves worst-group accuracy further at the last layer (Table~\ref{tab:lit})---but it is a
substantial change to the representation, which is what this section needs.
Reweighting did not help ($0.466$ and $0.364$, both a little below ERM). We keep it: a
representation that fails to improve is still a representation that changed.

\paragraph{Coverage under the two rules.} Table~\ref{tab:repr} and Figure~\ref{fig:repr} show the result. Under marginal calibration the
representation matters a great deal: worst-group coverage follows worst-group accuracy across the
three fine-tuned models and spans $0.249$ on Waterbirds. Under Mondrian the same three land within
$0.004$ of each other, and the calibration switch alone lifts the weakest to join the others. CelebA
repeats the pattern on a smaller scale, because its marginal coverage was never far from target.

The dissociation is therefore not an artefact of freezing the backbone. Even when the
representation is genuinely improved, switching the calibration rule buys more
worst-group coverage than switching the representation, and group-conditional
calibration removes almost all of the difference between representations. What retraining does buy shows up in the accuracy column, as in Section~\ref{sec:c2}.

\subsection{Set Size under Group-Conditional Calibration}
\label{sec:c2}

If Mondrian equalizes worst-group \emph{coverage} across training methods, where does
training help? Table~\ref{tab:c2} reports worst-group set size under Mondrian, where coverage is
matched, against worst-group accuracy. The direction agrees with an efficiency reading, with
exceptions. In four of eight settings the most accurate retained method also gives the smallest
worst-group sets, and in three of the four that miss it is within $0.010$ of the smallest. ERM
gives the largest sets in three of the six settings where it is retained. The
exceptions matter. On ResNet-50/Waterbirds and CLIP/Waterbirds AFR produces the largest sets
($1.369$ and $1.342$ against ERM's $1.110$ and $1.278$), and on DINOv2/Waterbirds GroupDRO-LL does
($0.954$ against $0.936$), so an
over-conservative method can be less efficient than the baseline it was meant to improve on.
ResNet-50/Waterbirds is also the one setting where ERM gives the smallest sets of all, by $0.005$,
and the only one where the most accurate method misses by a real margin ($0.070$).

How far that direction can be pushed is limited by how few methods each setting contains.
A per-setting correlation is computed over three to five methods, and with a bootstrap interval
over those methods six of the eight span essentially $[-1,+1]$. A seventh, ViT-B/16 on CelebA,
rests on three methods, where most resamples hold two distinct points and return $\pm1$, so its
narrow interval is no more informative. Coefficients of $-0.97$ and $-1.00$ in two CelebA settings
are, at $n=4$, not evidence of a deterministic relationship. No interval excludes zero except that
three-method one, and the eighth setting, ResNet-50/Waterbirds, is the only positive coefficient
with an interval narrower than the full range and it still contains zero. We therefore report a direction, and only under Mondrian.
Under one shared threshold training still moves coverage, so the efficiency reading holds only once
calibration is group-conditional.

The differences in efficiency are themselves substantial, from $0.022$ to $0.259$ across
settings, and in six of the eight the same check leaves the spread above a margin of $0.10$. Like
the $0.05$ used for coverage, that margin was chosen after seeing the spreads. Here it works against
us: a wider margin makes equivalence easier to accept, and the check still does not accept it. Training changes how large the worst group's sets are,
while calibration decides whether that group is covered at all.

On DINOv2/Waterbirds the mean worst-group set size is \emph{below} one for every training
method and every score ($0.890$--$0.954$), so a small fraction of prediction sets are empty. This is
a legitimate outcome of split conformal with a highly accurate model, since the calibration quantile
can fall below every class score, and coverage there remains valid under Mondrian
($0.857$--$0.882$). We do not clip sets to a minimum size of one, because that would hide it.

\subsection{Divergence at Matched Accuracy}
\label{sec:h1}

If the calibration rule governs coverage, a training-side reduction in cross-group score
divergence should be hard to tell apart from an accuracy effect. In seven of eight settings it
cannot be told apart at all.
Figure~\ref{fig:h1} and Table~\ref{tab:h1} show the confound: ERM's raw APS divergence
of $0.05$--$0.22$ falls to $0.01$--$0.12$ for the robust methods, and base accuracy
moves with it.

Separating the two requires matching at model level, with one accuracy per training seed and a
two-stage cluster bootstrap that resamples seeds and then splits within them. Matching on the
split-level spread would instead compare ERM on unlucky evaluation draws against a robust method on
lucky ones (Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}). At model level ERM's accuracy support collapses to a single
\emph{point}. It overlaps a robust method's support in exactly one of the eight settings,
GroupDRO-LL on ResNet-50/Waterbirds, whose per-seed accuracies bracket ERM's $0.9399$. There the
reduction is real: $\Delta_{\mathrm{APS}}=+0.084\,[+0.064,+0.101]$,
$\Delta_{\mathrm{RAPS}}=+0.084\,[+0.063,+0.101]$ and
$\Delta_{\mathrm{THR}}=+0.150\,[+0.129,+0.168]$, with all three intervals excluding zero. Six
arms that appear matched on the split-level spread, among them the two the submitted version
reported, GroupDRO-LL on CLIP/Waterbirds and balanced subsampling on ResNet-50/Waterbirds, are
unmatched once accuracy is read per model.

That the comparison is usually unavailable is the more telling result. On CelebA the robust methods raise base accuracy to $0.81$--$0.91$ against ERM's
$0.68$--$0.70$, so no common operating point exists. On the remaining Waterbirds settings the robust
methods straddle ERM, from $0.001$ above its accuracy to $0.149$ below, but no single method's
per-seed support covers it. Across most of the grid, an uncontrolled comparison of $D$ reports an
accuracy difference.

The two rankings nonetheless agree. Table~\ref{tab:h2} pairs the accuracy-best and shortfall-best method in each setting. The point
estimates differ in five of eight, but only one difference is separated from zero, by $0.014$ of
coverage. Worst-group accuracy is therefore a serviceable guide to the worst group's coverage
shortfall, though the two need not coincide.

\subsection{Correlation Shift and Deployment}
\label{sec:h3}

Three deployment conditions could undo the result: a change in how strongly the spurious
attribute is expressed, an audit that recovers the protected group, and calibration without group
labels. The first two leave it intact; the third does within a scope we state.

\paragraph{Scope of the sweep.} The $\rho$ sweep changes the
\emph{group mixture} of the evaluation distribution---the probability that the
spurious attribute aligns with the label---while the class-conditional feature
distribution within each of the four groups is untouched. It is therefore a
group-prior shift, a special case of label-and-attribute shift, and \emph{not} a
general covariate shift: it does not move the within-group data distribution, corrupt
inputs, or change the domain. Our stability claim is scoped accordingly. It says that
group-conditional calibration is insensitive to how strongly the spurious correlation is expressed
at deployment, and nothing about robustness to within-group distribution change, which we do not
test. On CelebA, where $\rho=0.95$ reverses the raw association (Section~\ref{sec:exp}), lowering
$\rhotest$ moves the evaluation distribution toward the raw data.

Figure~\ref{fig:shift} sweeps $\rhotest$ with calibration fixed at $\rhocal=0.95$. Worst-group
coverage is flat across the sweep. Under APS and Mondrian it varies by at most $0.024$ for every method and
setting, and under one shared threshold by at most $0.037$. This is expected, since a group-prior
shift leaves each group's coverage under a fixed threshold unchanged. The backbone also matters far less under
Mondrian: at the calibration point its envelope across backbones is $0.014$ wide on Waterbirds,
against $0.121$ under one shared threshold. Shift-robust calibration, whose threshold grows with the
calibration-to-test distance, gains up to $0.347$ under APS as $\rhotest$ falls by design, and degenerates
under THR on CelebA (Appendix~\ref{app:supp}).

Under Mondrian no method reaches $0.90$: the level is $0.84$--$0.89$ under APS and $0.83$--$0.89$
once RAPS and THR are included, so group-conditional calibration relocates the worst-group gap
without closing it. At the calibration point a strict bar of $0.88$ is missed on average on both datasets, narrowly
on CelebA ($0.8799$) and clearly on Waterbirds ($0.866$), and cleared by six of CelebA's fourteen
kept (setting, method) pairs. The
curve is flat in both, so this is a sub-target level, not a collapse under shift.

The sweep shows no relocation either. The matched-divergence reduction of Section~\ref{sec:h1}
becomes undefined, and set-size disparity barely moves between $\rhotest=0.95$ and $0.5$: under APS it grows
in seven of the thirty-four retained (setting, method) pairs, never by more than $0.011$, and falls
by at most $0.034$. The ``relocate, not remove'' effect of \citet{burden2026} therefore does not
appear as a shift effect.

The sub-target level is a finite-sample effect, and two quantities show it. Over the three methods
whose seeds differ, which Table~\ref{tab:variance} averages, Mondrian's split-to-split standard
deviation in worst-group coverage is $0.021$--$0.036$ on Waterbirds against $0.011$--$0.019$ on
CelebA, and its shortfall from target follows the same order, $0.029$--$0.043$ against
$0.016$--$0.023$. A shortfall that shrinks with per-group quantile noise is what
finite-sample quantile estimation produces: the minority groups hold a few percent of each
calibration set, and CelebA's pools are the larger ones. Mondrian's advantage over one shared threshold lies in the \emph{level} it attains, not
in its variance: marginal's split-to-split SD is $0.007$--$0.043$, comparable to Mondrian's, and in
five of the eight settings Mondrian is the less stable of the two
(Table~\ref{tab:variance}).

\paragraph{Group auditing.}
\label{sec:audit}

The representation on which worst-group coverage is obtained does not hide the protected group. A
standardized probe recovers the spurious attribute with $\auroc\in[0.945,0.999]$ in all eight
settings, while Mondrian delivers worst-group coverage of $0.83$--$0.89$ for every kept method and
score. Coverage and post-hoc group auditing therefore coexist. Projecting out the top-$k$
spurious-predictive directions ($k\in\{0,1,5,20\}$) tests the converse, for ResNet-50 and CLIP on
both datasets. At $k=20$ the probe falls to $\auroc\in[0.49,0.64]$ while worst-group coverage moves
by at most $0.022$: by less than $0.005$ on CelebA, and at most on CLIP/Waterbirds, from $0.854$ to
$0.876$, which clears the $0.02$ bar our auditability test uses to flag a tradeoff but stays $0.024$
short of target. Forcing invariance does not unlock coverage, which group-conditional calibration
already delivers from a representation that stays fully recoverable.

\paragraph{Predicted groups.}
\label{sec:predgroup}

Mondrian needs group labels, which a deployment lacks, but the spurious attribute is easy to
recover from the deployed features. Only the attribute is predicted, never the label $y$. A probe fitted on the training
split's features predicts $\hat a$. Calibration points are binned by $\hat g=2y+\hat a$, whose $y$
is known there, and a test point admits candidate label $c$ when its score falls below
$\hat q_{2c+\hat a}$, so the predicted attribute is all the test side uses. Coverage is always scored against the
\emph{true} group, so a mis-assignment counts against us. The attribute is then needed on the
training split alone, not on the calibration split and never at test. The requirement is weakened,
not removed, since $y$ is still needed to calibrate, as in any split-conformal method.

Table~\ref{tab:predgroup} compares this deployable version with the ground-truth oracle. The
median coverage gap per setting is $0.001$--$0.012$, and $30$ of $34$ methods stay within $0.02$ of
the oracle, the same count under RAPS but not under THR, so we scope the claim to those two scores
(Appendix~\ref{app:supp}). The penalty is asymmetric by construction. The minority stratum carries the largest
threshold, so a minority point misplaced into a majority stratum is judged too strictly and
under-covered, while the reverse error only wastes efficiency. Probe accuracy does not order the
failures: the setting with the lowest $\auroc$ has none, and one at $\auroc=0.999$ has one. The
penalty follows the method's own group-dependence instead. The largest failure is ERM's and the
next is GroupDRO-LL's, whose minority points sit closest to the decision boundary. The probe's
$\auroc$ is therefore necessary alongside any such claim, but not sufficient
(Appendix~\ref{app:supp}).

\paragraph{Ethical scope.} This version predicts a protected attribute for individuals. CelebA's
\texttt{Male} field is a binary annotation applied by third parties to photographs, not
self-identified gender, and the stratum it induces should be read as ``the annotation that
co-varies with hair colour in this dataset'', nothing more. Inferring such an attribute at test time
is also a different act from conditioning on one a person has disclosed. It assigns a sensitive
label to someone who has not provided it, and for some of them it assigns the wrong one, even at the
$\auroc$ we measure. We therefore scope the recommendation. Predicted-group Mondrian is appropriate where the
attribute is already lawfully held, or where the inference stays inside an audit an operator runs on
their own system and never reaches a per-person decision. It is not appropriate where the inferred
label would be stored, disclosed or used to route individual outcomes. There the true-group version
should be used, with attributes obtained by consent. Regimes
governing special-category data may prohibit the inference outright, whatever its
accuracy.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our results recommend group-conditional calibration wherever the worst group's coverage
matters, and they credit training with smaller sets only once calibration is group-conditional.
One cost of that recommendation points the other way.
\citet{cresswell2024} give prediction sets to human decision-makers and find that
equalizing coverage produces the \emph{largest} disparity in outcomes of any rule they
test, because it widens the gap in set sizes; they recommend equalizing set sizes instead. Our
data reproduces their mechanism. Moving from a shared threshold to per-group thresholds raises the
set-size disparity in six of the eight settings, and leaves the range at $0.132$--$0.323$ against
$0.080$--$0.339$ (Table~\ref{tab:pergroupcov}). In the setting of Figure~\ref{fig:overview}(d), the
worst group's sets grow from $1.080$ to $1.278$, and the average grows with them, from $0.984$ to
$1.214$.

Both results stand, and what
separates them is which quantity carries the guarantee. Where coverage is what is promised, as in an
audit, a service-level agreement or a regulatory floor, the calibration rule is the lever, and the
cost is paid in set size. Who pays depends on the setting: on Waterbirds the average grows in all
four settings, by up to $0.151$, and the worst group's sets in three of them, while on CelebA,
where the group that comes out worst is one of the two large aligned cells, the average shrinks in
all four and the worst group's sets in three. Where a person reads the set and acts
on it, set size is what reaches the decision, and equalizing coverage buys validity at a price paid
in the wrong currency.

Three things follow for practice. First, compare cross-group divergence \emph{at matched
accuracy}, and treat an uncontrolled comparison as uninformative, not as a smaller version of the
same effect. In the one setting where matching is possible the controlled estimate is not smaller
than the uncontrolled one, and in the other seven no controlled estimate exists. Second, a method
chosen for worst-group accuracy is rarely a poor choice for the coverage shortfall. Third, take
worst-group coverage from the calibration rule. It works on ordinary, fully auditable
representations and, because the group is recoverable, needs no group labels at test time.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

This study has six limitations. First, the evidence comes from two vision benchmarks with binary
labels and binary spurious attributes, four frozen backbones and one backbone fine-tuned
end-to-end. With two classes a set holds at most two labels, so the efficiency axis is compressed,
although the observed spread across training methods still runs from $0.022$ to $0.259$. A third
dataset and a multi-class task would show whether the dissociation depends on $|\mathcal{Y}|=2$.
Second, the fine-tuning study moves the representation for one architecture only, so its evidence is
narrower than the frozen grid's. Third, the accuracy-matched comparison is undefined wherever a
method's accuracy support does not overlap ERM's, which prevents a uniform statement. Fourth,
Mondrian's group-conditional coverage is close to a per-group target by construction, so the
non-trivial content is the \emph{magnitude} of marginal calibration's representation-dependent
failure and the size of the ERM lift. Fifth, deployable Mondrian (Section~\ref{sec:predgroup}) holds
in thirty of thirty-four (setting, method) pairs under APS and again under RAPS---the four misses
are two ERM, one AFR and one GroupDRO-LL---and degrades under THR, where nine pairs miss. It also infers a protected attribute at test time, so it should be used only where that
inference is permissible and auditable. Sixth, the confidence intervals do not model cross-dataset
variance.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Worst-group conformal coverage depends on the calibration rule far more than on the
representation, and the difference is large enough to change practice. Giving the worst group its
own threshold lifts an ordinary model by up to $0.345$, and once every group has one the choice of
training method moves worst-group coverage by at most $0.024$ under APS. Robust training still earns its place, because once calibration is
group-conditional it buys smaller sets, but it should not be credited with reliability that
calibration provides. We release the full grid, the representation study, the shift
sweep and the matched-accuracy protocol, so that training-side conformal claims can be
checked without the accuracy confound.

This appendix proves Proposition~\ref{prop:dissociation}, reports which group attains the minimum

\subsection*{Proposition~\ref{prop:dissociation}}

\begin{proof}
(i) is the standard finite-sample split-conformal guarantee applied within each
group under within-group exchangeability. For (ii), the group-$g$ coverage is
$F_g(\hat q)$ by definition of $C$. Writing the pooled CDF as the mixture
$F=\pi F_{\wg}+(1-\pi)F_{\neg\wg}$ and using $F(\hat q)=1-\alpha$,
\[
1-\alpha=\pi F_{\wg}(\hat q)+(1-\pi)F_{\neg\wg}(\hat q)
        =F_{\wg}(\hat q)+(1-\pi)\big[F_{\neg\wg}(\hat q)-F_{\wg}(\hat q)\big],
\]
which rearranges to \eqref{eq:shortfall}. Inequality \eqref{eq:ksbound} follows since
$F_{\neg\wg}(\hat q)-F_{\wg}(\hat q)\le\sup_t|F_{\wg}(t)-F_{\neg\wg}(t)|=D_{\mathrm{KS}}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Expected minimum over groups}

Appendix~\ref{app:subtarget} uses the approximation
$\mathbb{E}[\min_g\cov_g]\approx(1-\alpha)-c_{|\mathcal{G}|}\,\sigma$, in which $c_k$ is minus the
expected minimum of $k$ standard normals: $-\mathbb{E}[\min_{j\le k}Z_j]=k\int
z\,\phi(z)\Phi(z)^{k-1}\,dz$, giving $c_2=0.564$, $c_3=0.846$, $c_4=1.029$ and $c_5=1.163$.
With four groups the reported minimum therefore sits about one per-group standard deviation below
the mean over groups even when every group is exactly valid. Treating the per-group coverages as independent and
Gaussian is an approximation---they share a test set, and coverage is an average of
Bernoulli draws---so the constant only sets the scale, and the value we compare against comes from
the simulation of Table~\ref{tab:subtarget}.

\subsection*{A construction for $\Wone$}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:ksbound} bounds the shortfall by $(1-\pi)D_{\mathrm{KS}}$ because
$D_{\mathrm{KS}}$ is a supremum over thresholds and the shortfall is the CDF gap at one
threshold. No such bound holds for $\Wone$. Fix the
pooled threshold $\hat q$ and a target gap $\delta$, and let
$F_{\neg\wg}(t)-F_{\wg}(t)$ equal $\delta$ at $t=\hat q$ but fall to zero outside
$[\hat q-\varepsilon,\hat q+\varepsilon]$. Then
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Wone(P_{\wg},P_{\neg\wg})
&= \int\big|F_{\wg}(t)-F_{\neg\wg}(t)\big|\,dt \\
&\le 2\varepsilon\delta \;\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to0]{}\; 0 ,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:w1zero}
\end{equation}
while the shortfall stays at $(1-\pi)\delta$ throughout. Eq.~\eqref{eq:w1zero} therefore drives
$\Wone$ arbitrarily small at fixed shortfall: it integrates the discrepancy over the line, while
the shortfall depends on it at a single point. We report $\Wone$ in
Table~\ref{tab:div} because it is the summary used by \citet{burden2026}, and use
$D_{\mathrm{KS}}$ wherever a bound or a prediction is claimed.

\section{Sub-Target Coverage under Mondrian}
\label{app:subtarget}

Mondrian's empirical worst-group coverage settles at $0.856$--$0.884$, averaged over methods,
rather than the nominal $0.90$ (Section~\ref{sec:c1}). This appendix shows the level is what an
\emph{exactly valid} procedure produces at our calibration sizes, so it is a property
of the reported statistic and not a validity failure.

Two effects combine, both finite-sample. The first is the statistic we report. Per-group split
conformal with the finite-sample-corrected quantile $\lceil(n_g+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$ covers at
$\ge1-\alpha$ \emph{in expectation over calibration draws}, but we report $\min_g\cov_g$, and the
minimum of $|\mathcal{G}|$ noisy coverages is biased low: for approximately Gaussian per-group
coverages with standard deviation $\sigma$, $\mathbb{E}[\min_g\cov_g]\approx
(1-\alpha)-c_{|\mathcal{G}|}\sigma$ with $c_4\approx1.03$.

The second is how much data each threshold rests on. Mondrian estimates each threshold from that
group's \emph{own} calibration sample, and the samples are unequal. On Waterbirds the two minority groups hold $2.5\%$ of the
calibration set each, a $19\times$ imbalance in nominal count against the aligned groups and
$5$--$12\times$ once distinct images are counted, the aligned $g_3$ being itself drawn $4.2\times$
over. On CelebA counting distinct images reverses the imbalance: the aligned $g_3$ is
blond-and-male, and its nominal $47.5\%$ share is $140$ distinct images drawn about fifty times
each, against $340$--$361$ for the two nominal minorities (Table~\ref{tab:data}), so the least
precisely estimated threshold there belongs to the group the composited counts make look the best
supported. Either way some threshold rests on far less information than its nominal count
suggests; \citet{ding2023classcond} study this regime and propose clustering classes to pool
calibration data when per-class counts are small.

Under the Mondrian rule the minimum is not tied to a particular kind of group
(Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}), so what follows is a law about the level rather than about one
group.
Figure~\ref{fig:subtarget} puts our own evaluations against it. Simulated at
our own per-group counts, an exactly valid procedure puts the minimum at $0.8702$ on
Waterbirds and $0.8847$ on CelebA; we observe $0.8660$ and $0.8799$, with $96.5\%$ and
$95.1\%$ of evaluations inside the simulated central range. Both simulations use $3000$ draws and
the nominal per-group counts rather
than the distinct-image counts, which understates the noise on exactly the groups the minimum tends
to fall on, so the residual of $0.004$--$0.005$ between the simulated and observed minima is an upper bound
on what the law leaves unexplained.
Table~\ref{tab:subtarget} repeats the exercise with no model, no data and no representation: four
groups, i.i.d.\ continuous scores, exact exchangeability, per-group conformal thresholds, and only
the realised per-group calibration counts varied.

without quite bracketing it, and the observed values track calibration size the same way, larger on
Waterbirds and smaller on the much larger CelebA pools. What settles the question is the unweighted mean over
groups, which Table~\ref{tab:pergroupcov} measures directly: $0.9024$ against a nominal $0.90$,
with the mean of the per-setting minima at $0.8728$. The $0.0296$ between them is the selection
effect of reporting a minimum, not under-coverage. We keep reporting $\min_g\cov_g$ because it is
the operationally relevant number, but always beside the mean so the two are not confused.

\section{Pre-Specified Hypotheses}
\label{app:hyps}

These are the six statements fixed before the experiments they concern
(Section~\ref{sec:exp}). Each could have come back negative, and we report whatever the data show.

\begin{hypothesis}[Coverage is a calibration property]\label{hyp:c1}
Under Mondrian, worst-group coverage is near target for every training method
including ERM, while under marginal split it falls below target by an amount that
depends on the training method---so the calibration policy governs worst-group
coverage far more than the representation does.
\end{hypothesis}
\begin{hypothesis}[Training buys efficiency]\label{hyp:c2}
Holding Mondrian fixed, worst-group set size differs by training method and tracks
base accuracy. \emph{As tested}, we read this against \emph{worst-group} accuracy rather than base
accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:c2}), which is the quantity the training methods are chosen to move. The
substitution was made when the analysis was written, and the hypothesis is left as it was fixed.
\end{hypothesis}
\begin{hypothesis}[Transfer]\label{hyp:h1}
Robust training lowers the accuracy-matched divergence relative to ERM (CI of
\eqref{eq:matched} excludes $0$ on $\ge 2/3$ scores at $\rhocal$).
\end{hypothesis}
\begin{hypothesis}[Ranking inversion]\label{hyp:h2}
The training method best for worst-group accuracy differs from the one best for
the worst group's coverage shortfall, with a CI-separated $\covgap$ gap.
\end{hypothesis}
\begin{hypothesis}[Shift survival]\label{hyp:h3}
Any reduction or coverage level holds across the $\rhotest$ sweep.
\end{hypothesis}
\begin{hypothesis}[No auditability tension]\label{hyp:audit}
The spurious attribute remains recoverable from the representation on which
worst-group coverage is obtained, so coverage and post-hoc group auditing coexist.
\end{hypothesis}

Table~\ref{tab:hypverdict} collects what each returned. Three came back split. Of the two clauses of
H\ref{hyp:c1}, the second, marginal split below target by a training-dependent amount, is confirmed,
judged by the across-method spread, in seven of the eight settings, all but ViT-B/16/CelebA, where the shared threshold already leaves
almost nothing to recover. The first, Mondrian near target for every method, is \textbf{not met} on the
C1 criterion fixed with these hypotheses, \emph{Mondrian within $0.02$ of target for every kept
training method, and marginal split significantly below target for every one of them}, which
Appendix~\ref{app:excl} reports met in one setting of eight. The dissociation the paper argues for
rests on the second clause and on the size of the spreads, not on Mondrian reaching target, which
Appendix~\ref{app:subtarget} explains it does not.

\paragraph{AFR on CelebA.} AFR reaches $0.686$--$0.890$ worst-group accuracy on Waterbirds but
$0.013$--$0.029$ on the three ViT backbones of CelebA, through a class-prior inversion: at the
default $\gamma=2$ the head predicts the positive class for $\approx\!86\%$ of examples where the
true rate is $\approx\!15\%$, so a \emph{majority} group becomes the worst. At $\gamma=1$ AFR's
worst-group accuracy rises above ERM's on all three, though still far below the floor. Because an
untuned hyperparameter would make the comparison unfair, we computed an \emph{oracle} upper bound,
the best $\gamma$ in $\{0.5,1,2,4\}$ chosen on the evaluation set, which no selection rule over that
grid could beat: $0.477$ on CLIP, $0.445$ on ViT-B/16, $0.427$ on ResNet-50 and $0.327$ on DINOv2,
all far below the $0.75$ floor. This separate re-run sits $0.007$ below the $0.434$ of
Table~\ref{tab:lit} at $\gamma=2$ on ResNet-50, a re-run difference of the size
Appendix~\ref{app:hyper} describes. AFR's exclusion is therefore a property of the method in this
regime, not of our choice of $\gamma$.

\paragraph{Restoring the excluded runs.} The exclusions are of two kinds. Of the three individual
seeds, all on CelebA/ResNet-50, only GroupDRO-LL seed $1$ falls in the three-seed calibration
comparison where per-group thresholds are evaluated; the two seed-$4$ runs, of GroupDRO-LL and
balanced subsampling, belong to the five-seed grid, which uses one shared threshold. Restoring seed
$1$ moves its setting's per-group spread from $0.0034$ to $0.0036$, far below the maximum of $0.024$
on Waterbirds, so the result is arithmetic rather than evidence. The informative number is the
method-level one below.

Restoring the whole excluded methods is the larger perturbation, and we recomputed every
result with them included. The dissociation is still unaffected: under Mondrian the
across-training spread rises only to $0.027$ at worst, against $0.024$ with the methods
excluded, while the marginal spread grows because the degenerate methods are themselves
badly under-covered. One thing does change and we report it. The pre-specified C1 criterion asks for Mondrian within
$0.02$ of target \emph{and} marginal significantly below target for \emph{every} method, and it is
met in exactly one of eight settings (CLIP/CelebA). That single setting \emph{flips} to failing
when the excluded AFR method is included, since AFR's per-group coverage there is $0.858$, short of the $0.88$ the criterion asks
for, while its marginal coverage of $0.695$ meets the other clause. The
criterion is therefore not robust to the exclusion rule, and we rest the claim on the size of the
spreads rather than on a pass/fail flag.

\section{Experimental Details}
\label{app:hyper}
The backbones, the five last-layer methods, the three scores, the correlation-strength grid and
the seed and split counts are those of Sections~\ref{sec:setup} and~\ref{sec:exp}; this appendix
gives the values and procedures they leave open.
\textbf{Calibration.} Mondrian fits one conformal quantile per group and pools nothing; a group
absent from the calibration sample would receive the full set, which never occurs here. Target
$1-\alpha=0.90$ throughout; the shift-robust rule is defined under ``Shift-robust procedure'' below.
\textbf{Matching level.} Matching is at model level, one accuracy per training seed. Pooling each
method's $(\mathrm{acc},D)$ points across seeds \emph{and} calibration splits would make the accuracy
axis mostly evaluation-draw noise: ERM's per-seed base accuracy is identical to five decimals in
every setting, so its apparent spread is only which evaluation rows were drawn, matching on it
compares ERM on unlucky draws against a robust method on lucky ones, and a bootstrap over the pooled
points treats nested rows as independent. Accuracy is overall (base) top-1 on the evaluation
distribution, and each robust method's $D(a^\star)$ is read off a least-squares line through its per-seed
points; ERM's is its mean, its accuracy being a single point.
\textbf{Recoverability.} Each direction the projection of Section~\ref{sec:audit} removes is the
normalised weight of a logistic probe refitted on the already deflated training features, and it is
removed from training and evaluation features alike.
\textbf{Calibration and test construction.} For each of $10$ split seeds the
evaluation set is partitioned once into disjoint halves by a seeded permutation; the
first half is the calibration reservoir and the second the test reservoir, so no row is
ever in both. Each reservoir is then resampled to its target correlation
strength---$\rhocal=0.95$ for calibration, $\rhotest$ for test---by drawing \emph{with
replacement} within that reservoir's own group indices, which is what makes an extreme $\rho$
reachable from a finite pool, and both draws are truncated to a common size
$n=\min(|\mathrm{cal}|,|\mathrm{test}|)$ so calibration and test sizes never differ
across $\rho$. The realised $\rho$ is logged per draw. Compositing also equalises the two classes, so CelebA's
evaluation distribution carries a blond rate of $50\%$ rather than the roughly $15\%$ of the raw
data. Randomised scores (APS, RAPS) take their tie-breaking uniforms from separate seeded streams
for calibration and test, and RAPS uses $\lambda=0.01$ with $k_{\mathrm{reg}}=1$.

\textbf{Per-group counts.} Waterbirds: train $4{,}795$, evaluation set $5{,}245$, with
the reweighting split holding $663/706/202/177$ for groups $2y+a=0,1,2,3$. CelebA: train

the $66$ and $373$ of Table~\ref{tab:data}---so every per-group threshold rests on at least $66$
calibration scores.

\textbf{Nominal and distinct counts.} The draw is with replacement, so a group's composited count
can exceed the distinct images behind it; Table~\ref{tab:data}'s last column gives both. The
minority groups are essentially subsampled, at $1.0$--$1.1$ rows per distinct image. The aligned
groups are inflated, mildly on Waterbirds, where $g_3$ draws $1{,}245$ rows from $294$ images, and
severely on CelebA, where blond-and-male $g_3$ draws $7{,}095$ rows from $140$
(Appendix~\ref{app:subtarget}). Both rules see the same composited distribution, so the comparison
is unaffected; the split-to-split variation of Table~\ref{tab:variance} is the part of the
uncertainty that carries this.
\textbf{Head hyperparameters.} Every head standardises features with the statistics of the split
it is fitted on, and every one but last-layer GroupDRO fits a multinomial logistic model with
L-BFGS, $C=1$ and at most $5000$ iterations. DFR and AFR are fitted on the held-out reweighting
split, ERM, balanced subsampling and GroupDRO-LL on the training split. DFR averages $10$
group-balanced subsamples; balanced subsampling
draws one; AFR uses $\gamma=2$, whose sensitivity is examined in
Appendix~\ref{app:excl}; last-layer GroupDRO runs $2000$ full-batch steps at learning
rate $0.05$ with group-weight step $\eta_q=0.05$ and $\ell_2$ penalty $10^{-3}$. The frozen
ResNet-50 backbone is the torchvision \texttt{IMAGENET1K\_V2} checkpoint trained in-domain with ERM
for $10$ epochs, with Adam at $10^{-3}$ and batch size $128$, on the full training split for
Waterbirds and on a random subsample of $30{,}000$ training images for CelebA; its features are then
fixed. Fine-tuning (Section~\ref{sec:repr}) starts from the torchvision
\texttt{IMAGENET1K\_V2} ResNet-50 checkpoint and uses Adam at
$10^{-3}$ for ERM and for reweighting, which samples groups at inverse frequency, SGD at $10^{-3}$ with weight decay $10^{-2}$ for
GroupDRO with momentum $0.9$ and group-weight step $\eta_q=0.05$, batch size $128$, mixed
precision, and the last epoch rather than a selected one. The epoch budget is $10$ epochs for
ERM and reweighting on Waterbirds against $20$ for GroupDRO, and $6$ for all three objectives on
CelebA, so GroupDRO has the longer schedule on Waterbirds only.
\textbf{Shift-robust procedure.} Let $\epsilon$ be the total-variation distance between
histograms of the calibration and test true-label scores over $50$ shared bins. The threshold is
the $\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha+\epsilon)\rceil$-th smallest of the $n$ calibration scores, or $+\infty$,
which returns the full set, when that index exceeds $n$. Raising the level by $\epsilon$ is what
keeps coverage at $1-\alpha$ for any test distribution within $\epsilon$ of the calibration
distribution in total variation.
\textbf{Seeds.} Training seeds $\{0,1,2,3,4\}$ for the grid and for the fine-tuning study on
Waterbirds, $\{0,1,2\}$ for the fine-tuning study on CelebA and for the calibration and
predicted-group ablations; calibration split seeds
$0$--$9$ throughout; bootstrap seeds fixed at $0$. Note that ERM and AFR are
deterministic given the cached features, so their across-seed variance is exactly zero.
\textbf{Compute.} The full grid and both ablations re-calibrate cached head posteriors,
and feature extraction runs once per backbone. The four-backbone grid, the calibration ablation
and the predicted-group study each take a few hours on a single machine, with no GPU
retraining.
\textbf{Reproducibility across machines.} The evaluation behind Table~\ref{tab:pergroupcov} was
re-run on a different machine from the one that produced the released records, on the same cached
features and the same released splits. DFR and last-layer GroupDRO reproduce exactly. ERM, AFR and
balanced subsampling differ in $305$ of the $2{,}400$ evaluations, by at most $0.013$ in worst-group
coverage, AFR holding $192$ of those. Each of the three is a single L-BFGS solve read at full
precision, and the same solve on the same features can stop a last bit apart under a different
linear-algebra build, which moves a few test points across a threshold; AFR carries that difference
twice, since a first head's probabilities become the sample weights of a second. Within either
machine the fits are deterministic, so ERM's and AFR's across-seed standard deviation is exactly
zero. The reported values are the released ones, the per-setting means agree to within $0.0004$,
and a reader re-running these three should expect the third decimal to move, occasionally the
second. The re-run measured coverage only, so all set sizes are the released ones.
