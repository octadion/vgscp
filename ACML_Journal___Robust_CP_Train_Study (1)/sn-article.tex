\abstract{%
Work on group robustness is measured almost entirely by worst-group accuracy, and it is
natural to assume that a model made accurate on its worst group will also give that
group reliable prediction sets---sets that contain the right label as often as they
promise.
We find that the two come apart. Across two spurious-correlation benchmarks and four
backbones, worst-group coverage depends far more on how the scores are turned into sets
than on how the model was trained: under one threshold shared by all groups, an ordinary
model covers $89\%$ of all test images but only $51\%$ of those in its worst group;
give each
group its own threshold and most of that gap closes under every training
method we tried, on the same model and the same scores. Fine-tuning the representation
end-to-end does not change this. What training does buy is smaller sets for the worst
group, and only once calibration is group-conditional. If the worst group has to be
reliable, the calibration rule is the place to start.
}
\keywords{conformal prediction, spurious correlation, group robustness, worst-group coverage, group-conditional calibration}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A waterbird standing on land is still a waterbird. A classifier trained on images
where waterbirds are nearly always over water will often disagree. Conformal prediction
\citep{vovk2005,angelopoulos2020,romano2020} is meant to keep such a model honest about
what it does not know: instead of committing to one label it returns a set, calibrated
so the true label falls inside it with a stated probability. Across the whole test set
it does what it promises. In one of the settings we study, the sets contain the right
species $89\%$ of the time against a $90\%$ target. For the birds on the wrong
background, the same sets contain it $51\%$ of the time.

The guarantee was never violated; it was marginal, and a marginal promise says nothing
about any particular group \citep{cresswell2024}. Two lines of work address the failure
underneath it and rarely meet. One changes how the model is trained, so that the worst
group becomes accurate. The other changes how the scores are calibrated, so that
coverage holds group by group. Section~\ref{sec:related} places our work against both.
They meet at an assumption that is widely held and rarely tested: that a representation
made accurate on its worst group will also make that group conformally reliable, so
robust training should pay off for the uncertainty estimates built on top of it.

It does not. Holding the model fixed and changing only the calibration rule moves
worst-group coverage further than any training method we tried, and the effect survives
fine-tuning the representation end-to-end. The size of the effect has a measurable source: the shortfall a shared
threshold produces is bounded by the Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance between the worst group's score
distribution and everyone else's. Across our eight settings that distance also tracks how much a
per-group threshold recovers, though with eight points in two dataset clusters we read that as an
association rather than a prediction.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The calibration rule moves worst-group coverage far more than the training
method does (Section~\ref{sec:c1}).} With the model held fixed, coverage spreads across training
methods by up to $0.358$ under one shared threshold and by at most $0.024$ under
per-group thresholds, in every one of eight backbone--dataset settings. The gap never
closes: the smallest lift from re-calibrating a fixed model, $+0.059$, is still more than twice the
largest spread across training methods, and every lift interval excludes zero. Fine-tuning the
backbone end-to-end does not change this (Section~\ref{sec:repr}), so it is not an artefact of
frozen features.

\item \textbf{The size of that effect tracks the score geometry
(Section~\ref{sec:c1}).} The shortfall obeys the bound of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ksbound} in every setting,
run by run and for whichever group is worst in that run. This checks the proposition of
Section~\ref{sec:setup} against our own data rather than establishing anything new, and the bound
is tight on Waterbirds and slack on CelebA.

\item \textbf{What training does buy is smaller sets, and only once calibration is
group-conditional (Section~\ref{sec:c2}).} With coverage matched across methods, the worst group's
set size still differs substantially between them---by $0.036$ to $0.364$, and a two-one-sided-test
check rejects equivalence at a $0.10$ margin in six of eight settings. Which method wins is only
loosely predicted by accuracy: the most accurate gives the smallest sets in six of eight settings
and ERM the largest in four of the six where it is retained, but the per-setting
accuracy--size correlations are uninformative in five of eight once an interval is attached. We
report the tendency, not a ranking rule.

\item \textbf{A protocol that separates the two, and how little survives it
(Section~\ref{sec:h1}).} Comparing cross-group divergence at matched model-level accuracy leaves
one of eight settings with overlapping support, because robust training moves base accuracy away
from ERM's operating point. This is a negative result about what can be measured: uncontrolled
comparisons credit training with a reduction that cannot be separated from an accuracy effect.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Group-robust training.} Worst-group accuracy is improved by
distributionally robust optimization \citep{sagawa2020}, last-layer retraining on
group-balanced data (DFR) \citep{kirichenko2023}, automatic feature reweighting
(AFR) \citep{qiu2023}, and pseudo-group methods such as JTT \citep{liu2021jtt} and
CNC \citep{zhang2022cnc}. The standard subpopulation-shift benchmark
\citep{yang2023change} compares these on accuracy-family metrics and calibration
error but reports no set-valued or coverage quantities. We take our last-layer methods from this literature so that our worst-group
accuracies are comparable with published ones; our object of study is what these interventions do to the \emph{conformal}
side, which this literature does not measure.

\paragraph{Group-conditional and fair conformal prediction.} Mondrian conformal prediction \citep{vovk2003mondrian,vovk2005} computes
group-conditional thresholds; conditional
guarantees beyond disjoint groups are given by \citet{gibbs2023} and
\citet{jung2023}, and \citet{deng2025kandinsky} extend group-conditional coverage
to overlapping and fractional memberships. On fairness, \citet{cresswell2024} show
that equalizing coverage can increase disparate impact and recommend equalizing
set sizes; \citet{afcp2024} adaptively equalize coverage while controlling
efficiency. Equalized coverage as a fairness standard for prediction sets is due to
\citet{romano2020equalized}, and \citet{burden2026} prove that it and equalized set
size cannot in general hold simultaneously, recasting the calibration policy as a
choice of \emph{where} cross-group heterogeneity manifests. Our work is the
empirical complement on the axis these papers hold fixed: we vary the
\emph{training} intervention (hence representation and score distribution) and ask
whether the conserved heterogeneity shrinks at the source. It does not; the
calibration policy remains decisive.

\paragraph{Conformal prediction under shift and for robust models.}
\citet{tibshirani2019} establish validity under covariate shift via likelihood
reweighting; multi-distribution robust conformal prediction \citep{mdcp2026}
targets uniform worst-case coverage as a group-DRO analogue \emph{inside the
calibration procedure}, holding the model fixed---consistent with our finding that
the calibration mechanism is the lever that matters. No prior work crosses the
training interventions with calibration rules under a correlation-strength
sweep, nor controls for the accuracy confound we isolate.

\paragraph{Uncertainty, concepts, and the opposite direction.} Some recent work
uses uncertainty to \emph{improve} group robustness---e.g.\ evidential alignment
that mitigates spurious bias without group labels \citep{evidential2025}---the
reverse of our direction. We treat conformal prediction over learned concept or
concept-bottleneck spaces \citep{conceptconf2025,ncb2024,ncv2025} as an
interpretability layer, orthogonal to our thesis. Selective classification under
group shift \citep{jones2021} found abstention can leave worst-group accuracy flat;
our set-valued analyses extend this lineage to conformal prediction.

\paragraph{Group recovery, conditional coverage, and its limits.} Whether the
protected group can be recovered is a separate question: biased ERM representations
encode the spurious attribute strongly (the principle behind JTT and CNC), which our
probe confirms in every setting (Section~\ref{sec:predgroup}). This connects to conformal prediction
under noisy class or group labels \citep{einbinder2022,sesia2023noisy}, which
corrects coverage but assumes the group is given; here the group is not noisy but
\emph{recoverable}, which removes the auditability barrier and, via predicted
groups, the deployment barrier (Section~\ref{sec:predgroup}). Finally, exact
feature-conditional coverage is
impossible distribution-free in finite samples \citep{lei2014distribution,barber2021limits}, which is
why group-\emph{conditional} (Mondrian) coverage is the target that matters; online
and multivalid variants \citep{gibbs2023,jung2023} tighten it, but our contribution
holds the calibration family fixed and varies the training intervention---on the last
layer and on the representation itself---to measure how little the representation
substitutes for the mechanism.

\section{Problem Setup and Notation}
\label{sec:setup}

We observe triples $(x,y,a)$ with image $x$, label $y\in\mathcal{Y}$, and binary
spurious attribute $a\in\{0,1\}$ (background/\emph{place} for Waterbirds;
\emph{Male} for CelebA). Groups are $g=(y,a)\in\mathcal{G}$,
$|\mathcal{G}|=2|\mathcal{Y}|$. A frozen backbone $\phi$ maps $x$ to features
$\phi(x)\in\mathbb{R}^d$; a last-layer head $f_\theta$ trained by intervention $m$
maps $\phi(x)$ to class scores. The \emph{correlation strength} $\rho$ is the
evaluation probability that $a$ aligns with $y$ in the majority pattern; we
calibrate at $\rhocal=0.95$ and evaluate over
$\rhotest\in\{0.95,0.9,0.8,0.7,0.6,0.5\}$, holding the calibration distribution
fixed.

\paragraph{Conformal sets and calibration policies.} Throughout, ``calibration''
means the conformal step that turns scores into a threshold, not the probability
calibration measured by expected calibration error \citep{guo2017calibration}. Given a nonconformity score
$s(x,y)$ and a calibration set $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$, split conformal
prediction computes
$\hat q=\mathrm{Quantile}_{1-\alpha}\big(\{s(x_i,y_i)\}_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}}\big)$
and returns $C(x)=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}: s(x,y)\le\hat q\}$. We use three scores: THR
($s_{\mathrm{THR}}(x,y)=1-\hat p(y\mid x)$); APS \citep{romano2020}, the cumulative
softmax mass up to $y$; and RAPS \citep{angelopoulos2020}, APS with a tail
regularizer. We compare three \emph{calibration policies}: \textbf{marginal split}
(one global $\hat q$); \textbf{Mondrian} (a separate $\hat q_g$ per group); and
\textbf{shift-robust} (a worst-case threshold over a total-variation ball around the calibration
distribution; Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}). Target $1-\alpha=0.90$
throughout; the full configuration is in Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}.

\paragraph{Reliability quantities.} For group $g$, $\cov_g=\Pr[y\in C(x)\mid g]$
and $|C|_g$ is its mean set size. We report worst-group coverage $\min_g\cov_g$
and the \emph{coverage gap}, i.e.\ the worst group's shortfall from the target,
\begin{equation}
\covgap=(1-\alpha)-\min_g\cov_g,
\label{eq:covgap}
\end{equation}
so $\covgap>0$ exactly when the worst group is under-covered. This is the quantity
tabulated throughout. We also report mean set size, the
set-size disparity
$\max_g|C|_g-\min_g|C|_g$, and---because $\min_g$ over a finite number of groups is
a downward-biased statistic (Section~\ref{sec:h3})---the unweighted mean over
groups $|\mathcal{G}|^{-1}\sum_g\cov_g$, which is the level group-conditional
calibration actually targets.

\paragraph{Cross-group score divergence.} Following the
conserved-quantity view of \citet{burden2026}, we summarize cross-group score
heterogeneity by the divergence between the worst group's conformity-score
distribution $P_{\wg}$ and that of the remaining groups $P_{\neg\wg}$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
D &= \Wone(P_{\wg},P_{\neg\wg}) \quad\text{(Wasserstein-1)}, \\
D_{\mathrm{KS}} &= \sup_t\big|F_{\wg}(t)-F_{\neg\wg}(t)\big| \quad\text{(Kolmogorov--Smirnov)},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:div}
\end{equation}
computed per score on a held-out split; $D$ is reported per score (scales differ).

\paragraph{What is true by construction, and what is not.} One elementary fact
separates what group-conditional calibration gives for free from the question we
test.

\begin{proposition}[Calibration--representation dissociation]
\label{prop:dissociation}
Fix a score $s$ and target $1-\alpha$, and assume within-group exchangeability of
calibration and test scores.
\emph{(i)} Group-conditional (Mondrian) split conformal, with per-group threshold
$\hat q_g$ the $\lceil(1-\alpha)(n_g+1)\rceil$-th smallest calibration score in
group $g$, satisfies $\Pr\!\big(Y\in C(X)\mid G=g\big)\ge 1-\alpha$ for every group
$g$, with no dependence on $s$ beyond exchangeability.
\emph{(ii)} Let the scores be continuous, let $\pi=\Pr(G=\wg)$ under the calibration
distribution, and let $F_g$ be the group-$g$ nonconformity-score CDF. Marginal split
conformal uses the single pooled threshold $\hat q$ solving
$\pi F_{\wg}(\hat q)+(1-\pi)F_{\neg\wg}(\hat q)=1-\alpha$, and has per-group coverage
$\Pr\!\big(Y\in C(X)\mid G=g\big)=F_g(\hat q)$. The worst-group shortfall then obeys
the exact identity
\begin{equation}
(1-\alpha)-F_{\wg}(\hat q)\;=\;(1-\pi)\,\big[F_{\neg\wg}(\hat q)-F_{\wg}(\hat q)\big],
\label{eq:shortfall}
\end{equation}
and consequently
\begin{equation}
(1-\alpha)-F_{\wg}(\hat q)\;\le\;(1-\pi)\,D_{\mathrm{KS}}.
\label{eq:ksbound}
\end{equation}
Hence the representation (through $s$, and through any training intervention that
changes $s$) controls worst-group coverage \emph{only} under marginal calibration;
under Mondrian it does not. \end{proposition}

\noindent The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. \citet{jones2021} decompose the
margin distribution the same way to ask when abstention helps the worst group: there the
shared threshold is a confidence cut and the quantity is accuracy, here it is a conformal
quantile and the quantity is coverage. Their negative answer and ours have one
source---a single threshold read against two different score distributions.

\noindent\textbf{Remark (what the divergences do and do not give).} It does not follow
from \eqref{eq:shortfall} that the shortfall grows with the divergence $D$ of
\eqref{eq:div}, and in general it does not. What is true: the shortfall is the
cross-group CDF gap \emph{at the threshold}, scaled by $(1-\pi)$, and $D_{\mathrm{KS}}$
bounds it because it is a supremum over $t$. $\Wone$ admits no such bound: the gap can
concentrate on a vanishing interval, sending $\Wone$ to zero while the shortfall stays
fixed (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}). We therefore use $\Wone$ only as a descriptive summary, its role in
\citet{burden2026}. Neither divergence orders coverage across models without assuming
the same group is worst in each, and we do not assume it: \eqref{eq:shortfall} and
\eqref{eq:ksbound} are applied run by run to whichever group is in fact worst. The
assumption is a good approximation for the ERM baseline under a shared threshold, where one group
is the worst in $85$--$100\%$ of runs, and a poor one under per-group thresholds, where no group
holds the minimum in more than $41\%$ of runs and the most concentrated setting is closer to the
$25\%$ of a four-way tie than to the shared-threshold figure (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}).

Part (i) holds by construction, so what we test is the size of the effect in part
(ii): how large the threshold-point gap of \eqref{eq:shortfall} is in practice, and
whether training shrinks it beyond what its accuracy gain explains
(Sections~\ref{sec:c1} and~\ref{sec:h1}). The identity also says when the shortfall falls hardest on a small group: it
scales with $(1-\pi)$, so a group holding little of the calibration mass is exposed. That is what
happens on Waterbirds, where the worst group under a shared threshold holds $2.5\%$ of the
calibration set. It is not what happens on CelebA: compositing to $\rhocal=0.95$ leaves the group
that comes out worst holding $47.5\%$, so there the shortfall is driven by the score geometry of
\eqref{eq:shortfall} rather than by a small $\pi$ (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}). The identity covers
both cases; the small-$\pi$ reading of it covers only the first.

Proposition~\ref{prop:dissociation} is a population statement, and the empirical
Mondrian level of $0.83$--$0.89$ sits below it for two finite-sample reasons: the
reported
statistic is a \emph{minimum} over $|\mathcal{G}|$ noisy per-group coverages, which is
biased low even when every group is individually valid; and under Mondrian each
group's threshold is estimated from that group's own calibration sample, which at
$\rhocal=0.95$ leaves the minority groups a few percent of the data. We quantify both
in Section~\ref{sec:h3} and Appendix~\ref{app:subtarget}, where the unweighted
mean-over-groups coverage is shown to sit at the nominal $0.90$.

\paragraph{Group recoverability.} To test whether the deployed representation
hides the protected group, we measure the test $\auroc$ of a probe
$h:\phi(x)\mapsto a$ trained in-domain to recover the spurious attribute.

\paragraph{Divergence at matched accuracy.} $D$ in \eqref{eq:div} and $\covgap$ in
\eqref{eq:covgap} are entangled with base accuracy: a more accurate model produces
sharper, more group-aligned score distributions, so ``robust training lowers $D$''
partly restates ``robust training raises accuracy''. For each method $m$ and the ERM
reference we collect per-seed pairs $\big(\mathrm{acc}^{(s)}_m,D^{(s)}_m\big)$ and read
the divergence at a common accuracy $a^\star$ in the \emph{overlapping} accuracy
support:
\begin{equation}
\Delta(a^\star)=D_{\mathrm{ERM}}(a^\star)-D_m(a^\star),
\label{eq:matched}
\end{equation}
with a $95\%$ bootstrap CI over seeds, so $\Delta(a^\star)>0$ means $m$ lowers the
divergence at matched accuracy. Where the supports do not overlap,
$\Delta(a^\star)$ is \textbf{undefined} and reported as \texttt{unmatched}; the raw $D$
appears separately, labelled uncontrolled.

\paragraph{Discipline.} We call one (backbone, dataset, training method, seed) tuple a
\emph{run}. Each run must reach the worst-group accuracy floor set for its training
method or it is excluded, with the reason recorded. Probes are standardised
(StandardScaler~$\to$~logistic regression) and trained in-domain, with no test-label
leakage. Probe training and calibration never see test labels.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp}

\paragraph{Datasets and backbones.} Waterbirds and CelebA (blond-hair
classification, \emph{Male} spurious). \emph{Four} frozen backbones give the breadth
axis, spanning two architecture families and three pretraining regimes: an ERM-trained
ResNet-50 ($d{=}2048$, the canonical setup), CLIP ViT-B/32 ($d{=}512$, image--text
contrastive), DINOv2 ViT-B/14 ($d{=}768$, self-supervised and never label-trained), and
a supervised ImageNet ViT-B/16 ($d{=}768$). Features are cached once per backbone.
Section~\ref{sec:repr} additionally fine-tunes the ResNet-50 end-to-end under three
objectives, so the representation itself moves, not only the head. Both tasks are binary
($|\mathcal{Y}|=2$) with four groups $g=(y,a)$; cached split sizes are in
Table~\ref{tab:data} and exact per-group counts are released with the artifacts.

\paragraph{Correlation strength.} For a target $\rho$ we resample the evaluation rows
so that the spurious attribute aligns with the majority label pattern with probability
$\rho$, holding the class balance fixed; the realised $\rho$ is logged per draw. Calibration uses $\rhocal=0.95$;
final-test coverage is read at each $\rhotest\in\{0.95,0.9,0.8,0.7,0.6,0.5\}$.

Mondrian falls back to the pooled quantile if a group's calibration count drops below
a minimum, which these runs never trigger (Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}).

\paragraph{Uncertainty.} Point estimates are means over five training seeds for the
main grid and the fine-tuning study, and three for the calibration comparison, each with
ten calibration splits. The comparison uses fewer seeds because it evaluates every
training method under three calibration rules, six correlation strengths and three
scores, and two of its five methods are deterministic in the seed anyway; the intervals
below are cluster bootstraps that account for the smaller number of clusters. Intervals are two-stage cluster bootstraps ($B=1000$) that
resample seeds and then splits within them, and Table~\ref{tab:variance} reports the
two standard deviations separately.

\paragraph{Training interventions (last-layer methods).} On frozen features we fit
ERM, DFR, AFR, balanced subsampling \citep{idrissi2022balancing}, and last-layer
GroupDRO (minutes each on cached
features). Section~\ref{sec:repr} covers the full-network case separately.

\paragraph{What the seed does not move.} ERM and AFR fit their heads with L-BFGS,
whose solver ignores the random seed, so they produce the same head every time: their
across-seed standard deviation is exactly $0.000$ and their base accuracy is identical
to five decimals in every setting. Extra seeds therefore add replicates for DFR,
balanced subsampling and GroupDRO-LL (across-seed SD $0.003$--$0.027$ in worst-group
accuracy) and none for ERM or AFR. This is also why every interval here is a cluster
bootstrap that resamples training seeds and then calibration splits within them: the ten
splits inside a seed share one trained model.

\paragraph{Which runs clear the floor.} Of the $200$ runs, $33$ fall below the
per-method worst-group accuracy floor and are excluded from the main analysis:
AFR on all four CelebA backbones (all seeds, $0.013$--$0.434$ against a floor of
$0.75$), ERM on the two CelebA settings with self-supervised and supervised ViT features
($0.203$ and $0.263$ against a floor of $0.30$), two GroupDRO-LL seeds and one
balanced-subsampling seed on CelebA/ResNet-50. Twelve further runs sit between their floor and a soft reference of $0.85$ on CelebA and
are \emph{kept}, flagged; that reference was set with the floors and, unlike them, is advisory---no
run is excluded by it. We fixed the six hypotheses of
Appendix~\ref{app:hyps} and these per-method floors before running any of the
experiments they concern, but did not lodge them in a public registry, so they are
pre-specified rather than preregistered. Every excluded method is still evaluated, so Appendix~\ref{app:excl} can report what
the results look like with them put back. Worst-group accuracy confirms what the floor is separating:
ERM is far below parity on the balanced distribution ($0.20$--$0.51$, strong shortcut
learning) while the robust methods reach $0.63$--$0.95$.

\paragraph{Comparison with published values.} Table~\ref{tab:lit} places our
ResNet-50 methods beside published worst-group accuracies for the same methods. The
gaps are large and the comparison is not like-for-like: we refit only the last layer on
features from a deliberately light recipe, where the published GroupDRO and AFR numbers
come from training the network, and we read accuracy on our pooled evaluation domain
(Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}). DFR, the one method whose protocol matches its published
counterpart, is correspondingly the closest, $0.851$ against $0.883$ on CelebA. Every
result in this paper is a \emph{within-setting} contrast---the same model and the same
posteriors, calibrated two ways---so a weaker backbone lowers all methods in a setting
together and leaves the calibration contrast intact; the four backbones span a
$0.20$--$0.95$ range of worst-group accuracy and behave alike.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The Calibration Rule Moves Coverage More Than Training Does}
\label{sec:c1}

Table~\ref{tab:c1} reports worst-group coverage under both calibration rules, for every
training method and setting. Under one shared threshold it follows the training method
and spreads by up to $0.358$; give each group its own threshold and the spread never
exceeds $0.024$, and a two-one-sided-test check finds it equivalent to zero within a
$0.05$ margin in all eight settings. That margin was not pre-specified---we chose it after seeing
the spreads, as a judgement about what coverage difference would be immaterial in
deployment---and it is wide relative to the spreads themselves, so the test bounds the effect
rather than resolving it; CLIP/Waterbirds comes closest to binding, at an upper bound of $0.042$,
so the result would be seven of eight at a margin of $0.04$. The pattern holds everywhere, and the
weakest method rises to meet the strongest.

The cleanest comparison is the same ERM model under the two rules
(Figure~\ref{fig:c1}). On CLIP/Waterbirds it moves from $0.509$ to $0.854$, matching
what DFR reaches, with nothing retrained. The gain is largest where the shared
threshold was failing worst and small where it was already close, and the two backbones
we added behave like the two we started with.

How large that gain will be is, to a first approximation, visible before any
re-calibration is done---though the two halves of that statement have different standing, and we
separate them.
Eq.~\eqref{eq:shortfall} says the shortfall is the cross-group gap between the two score
distributions at the threshold, scaled by $1-\pi$, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:ksbound} bounds it
by $(1-\pi)D_{\mathrm{KS}}$. The bound is satisfied in all eight settings (Figure~\ref{fig:mech}, left), and there it
is applied run by run, to whichever group that run's shared threshold leaves worst. It is not
uniformly tight: the shortfall reaches $0.59$--$0.93$ of the bound on Waterbirds but only
$0.43$--$0.58$ on CelebA, so the bound is informative where the effect is large and slack where it
is small. The right panel
is a weaker kind of claim and we mark it as such: $D_{\mathrm{KS}}$, measured under the shared
threshold and averaged over training methods, tracks the gain a per-group threshold delivers with
$r=0.93$. That is an ordering of coverage by divergence \emph{across} models, which
Section~\ref{sec:setup}'s remark says no divergence licenses on its own; it is offered as an
observed association over eight points---which fall into two dataset clusters, and for which we
quote no interval---not as a prediction the bound underwrites. Where the two groups' score distributions nearly coincide, as on
CelebA with the self-supervised and supervised ViT features, there is little to recover
and re-calibration changes almost nothing. The dissociation is not a property of the
backbones we happened to choose; it follows the score geometry, and the score geometry
is measurable in advance.

\subsection{Retraining the Representation}
\label{sec:repr}

Every result so far holds the backbone fixed and varies the last layer, so on its own
it cannot separate ``calibration beats last-layer adaptation'' from ``calibration
beats robust training in general''. This section reports the stronger test: we
fine-tune the backbone \emph{end-to-end} under three objectives---plain ERM,
GroupDRO, and group reweighting---and repeat the comparison on the representations
that result, with five fine-tuning seeds per objective and the head held fixed at
plain ERM so that the representation is the only thing that moves. How much of a pretrained
network to unfreeze changes what the resulting representation encodes, and full
fine-tuning can distort pretrained features relative to linear probing
\citep{kumar2022finetuning,elmehdi2024unfreezing}. We unfreeze all of it, the setting in
which the representation moves most, which pulls it as far as each objective
allows and gives calibration the hardest test our setting can offer.

\paragraph{Manipulation check.} A representation-level experiment is informative only
if the intervention actually moved the representation, so we verify that first.
GroupDRO fine-tuning raises worst-group accuracy from $0.516$ to $0.738$ on
Waterbirds ($+0.222$) and from $0.396$ to $0.470$ on CelebA ($+0.074$), the largest
gain any of our representation-level objectives produces. It is not the largest in the paper---DFR
moves worst-group accuracy further at the last layer (Table~\ref{tab:lit})---but it is a
substantial change to the representation, which is what this section needs.
Reweighting did not help ($0.466$ and $0.364$, both a little below ERM). We keep it: a
representation that fails to improve is still a representation that changed.

\paragraph{Both changes at once.} Table~\ref{tab:repr} and Figure~\ref{fig:repr} contrast them. Under marginal calibration the representation matters a great deal: on
Waterbirds, worst-group coverage spans $0.249$ across the three fine-tuned models.
Under Mondrian the same three land within $0.004$ of each other, and the weakest of
them by worst-group accuracy is lifted to join the others by the calibration switch
alone. CelebA repeats the pattern on a smaller scale, for the same reason as in
Section~\ref{sec:c1}: its marginal coverage was never far from target to begin with.

The dissociation is therefore not an artefact of freezing the backbone. Even when the
representation is genuinely improved, switching the calibration rule buys more
worst-group coverage than switching the representation, and group-conditional
calibration removes almost all of the difference between representations. What retraining does buy shows up in the accuracy column, as in Section~\ref{sec:c2}.

\subsection{What Training Buys Instead: Smaller Sets}
\label{sec:c2}

Given that Mondrian equalizes worst-group \emph{coverage} across training methods,
where does training help? Table~\ref{tab:c2} reports worst-group set size under
Mondrian---where coverage is matched---against worst-group accuracy. The direction
agrees with an efficiency reading, though not without exceptions: in six of eight
settings the most accurate retained method also yields the smallest worst-group sets, and ERM
yields the largest in four of the six settings where it is retained. The exceptions are
instructive rather than incidental---and the AFR margin below is an order of magnitude
larger than the reproduction discrepancy of Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}. On ResNet-50/Waterbirds it is AFR that produces the largest
sets ($1.217$ against ERM's $1.138$) and on DINOv2/Waterbirds GroupDRO-LL does ($0.962$ against
$0.927$), so a degenerate or over-conservative method can be less efficient than the baseline it
was meant to improve on. The two settings where the most accurate method is not the most efficient
are both ResNet-50, and on CelebA the margin is $0.001$.

How far that direction can be pushed is limited by how few methods each setting contains.
A per-setting correlation is computed over three to five methods, and with a cluster-bootstrap
interval five of the eight span essentially $[-1,+1]$; coefficients as extreme as
$-0.97$ and $-1.00$ turn up in two CelebA settings, which at $n=4$ is not evidence of a
deterministic relationship. Three settings have intervals excluding zero---two on Waterbirds
and one on CelebA---and all three are negative, the direction an efficiency reading
predicts; the two positive coefficients both carry intervals spanning $[-1,+1]$. We therefore report a direction, and only under Mondrian: under one shared threshold training still moves coverage, so the efficiency reading holds only once calibration is group-conditional.

The differences in efficiency are themselves substantial, ranging from $0.036$ to
$0.364$ across settings, and a two-one-sided-test check rejects equivalence at a margin of
$0.10$ in six of the eight. That margin, like the $0.05$ used for coverage, was chosen after seeing
the spreads; here it works against us, since a wider margin makes equivalence easier to accept and
we reject it anyway. Training changes how large the worst group's sets are,
while calibration decides whether that group is covered at all.

One number needs disclosure. On DINOv2/Waterbirds the mean worst-group set size is
\emph{below} one for every training method and every score ($0.888$--$0.962$), which means a small
fraction of prediction sets are empty. That is a legitimate outcome of standard split conformal
with a highly accurate model---the calibration quantile can fall below every class
score---and coverage there remains valid under Mondrian ($0.857$--$0.882$). Clipping sets to a minimum size of one would hide this, so we do not.

\subsection{The Apparent Transfer Cannot Be Separated from Accuracy}
\label{sec:h1}

If the calibration rule is what governs coverage, then any training-side reduction in
cross-group score divergence should be hard to tell apart from an accuracy effect. It is---and in
seven of eight settings it cannot be told apart at all.
Figure~\ref{fig:h1} and Table~\ref{tab:h1} show the confound: ERM's raw APS divergence
of $0.05$--$0.22$ falls to $0.01$--$0.12$ for the robust methods, and base accuracy
moves with it.

Separating the two requires matching at model level---one accuracy per training seed,
with a two-stage cluster bootstrap that resamples seeds and then splits within them.
Matching on the split-level spread instead would compare ERM on unlucky evaluation draws
against a robust method on lucky ones, for reasons Appendix~\ref{app:hyper} sets out.
At model level ERM's accuracy support collapses to a single \emph{point}, and it
overlaps a robust method's in exactly one of the eight settings: GroupDRO-LL on
ResNet-50/Waterbirds, whose per-seed accuracies bracket ERM's $0.9399$. There the
reduction is real---$\Delta_{\mathrm{APS}}=+0.084\,[+0.076,+0.095]$,
$\Delta_{\mathrm{RAPS}}=+0.084\,[+0.074,+0.094]$,
$\Delta_{\mathrm{THR}}=+0.150\,[+0.146,+0.158]$, all three intervals excluding zero.
Two settings that appear matched on the split-level spread---GroupDRO-LL on
CLIP/Waterbirds and balanced subsampling on ResNet-50/Waterbirds---are unmatched once
the accuracy is read per model.

That the comparison is usually unavailable is the more telling result. On CelebA the
robust methods raise base accuracy to $0.81$--$0.91$ against ERM's $0.68$--$0.70$, so
no common operating point exists; on the remaining Waterbirds settings the robust
methods straddle ERM, from $0.1$ point above its accuracy to $14.9$ below, without any single
method's per-seed support covering it. Across most of the grid, an
uncontrolled comparison of $D$ is reporting an accuracy difference.

The two rankings nonetheless agree. Table~\ref{tab:h2} pairs the accuracy-best and
shortfall-best method in each setting: the point estimates differ in five of eight, but
only one difference is separated from zero, and there by $0.014$ of coverage. Worst-group
accuracy is a serviceable guide to the worst group's coverage shortfall, with the
caveat that the two need not coincide.

\subsection{When Deployment Differs from Calibration}
\label{sec:h3}

The remaining question is whether the dissociation survives a change in how
strongly the spurious attribute is expressed at deployment.

\paragraph{What this sweep does and does not vary.} The $\rho$ sweep changes the
\emph{group mixture} of the evaluation distribution---the probability that the
spurious attribute aligns with the label---while the class-conditional feature
distribution within each of the four groups is untouched. It is therefore a
group-prior shift, a special case of label-and-attribute shift, and \emph{not} a
general covariate shift: it does not move the within-group data distribution, corrupt
inputs, or change the domain. Our stability claim is scoped accordingly. It says that
group-conditional calibration is insensitive to how strongly the spurious correlation
is expressed at deployment---the failure mode this literature is concerned with---and
it says nothing about robustness to within-group distribution change, which would need
a different construction and which we do not test.

We sweep $\rhotest$ with calibration fixed at $\rhocal=0.95$
(Figure~\ref{fig:shift}; full tables in Appendix~\ref{app:supp}). Three observations
hold across settings. (i) \emph{Stability}: under Mondrian, worst-group coverage varies
by at most $0.024$ over the whole sweep for every method and setting, and under marginal
split by at most $0.037$. We scope this to those two policies: shift-robust
calibration inflates its level with the observed calibration-to-test distance by
construction, so its coverage rises as $\rhotest$ falls---by up to $0.347$ across the
sweep---which is intended behaviour rather than instability under APS and RAPS. Under
THR on CelebA the same rule degenerates instead, returning the set of both labels and a coverage of
$1.000$ that means nothing; we read that column as a failure mode and set it out in
Appendix~\ref{app:supp}. (ii) \emph{Sub-target}: under Mondrian no
method reaches $0.90$; the stable level is $0.84$--$0.89$ under APS ($0.83$--$0.89$ once RAPS and THR are included), consistent with
group-conditional calibration relocating but not closing the worst-group gap.
Whether this level clears a strict ``$\ge\!0.88$'' validity bar across the sweep is
therefore baseline-dependent: averaged over methods it does on CelebA (level $\approx0.88$) and does not on Waterbirds
(level $\approx0.87$), though per method CelebA clears it in only seven of fourteen kept cells;
the curve is flat in both---this is a
sub-target baseline, not a shift collapse. (iii) \emph{No relocation}: the H3
matched-divergence reduction becomes \texttt{undefined} across the sweep, and set-size disparity \emph{eases} as $\rho\!\to\!0.5$ in most settings (only DFR and GroupDRO-LL on CelebA/ResNet-50 show mild growth). The
``relocate, not remove'' phenomenon of \citet{burden2026} is therefore not
observed as a shift effect: the divergence simply tracks correlation strength and is largest at the calibration point.

The sub-target level is a finite-sample effect, and two quantities scale together to show it. Mondrian's split-to-split standard deviation in
worst-group coverage is $0.021$--$0.036$ on Waterbirds against $0.011$--$0.019$ on
CelebA, and its shortfall from target follows the same ordering---$0.030$--$0.044$
against $0.016$--$0.023$. A shortfall that shrinks with the per-group quantile noise is the signature of finite-sample worst-group quantile estimation: the minority groups hold a few percent of each calibration set, and CelebA's
pools are the larger ones. Mondrian's advantage over one shared threshold lies in the \emph{level} it attains, not
in its variance: marginal's split-to-split SD is $0.007$--$0.043$, comparable to Mondrian's, and in
five of the eight settings Mondrian is the less stable of the two
(Table~\ref{tab:variance}).

\paragraph{Coverage and Group Auditing Coexist}
\label{sec:audit}

The representation on which worst-group coverage is obtained does not hide the protected group. A standardized probe recovers the spurious attribute at
$\auroc\in[0.945,0.999]$ across all eight settings while Mondrian simultaneously
delivers worst-group coverage of $0.83$--$0.89$ across every kept method and score:
coverage and post-hoc group auditing coexist. Projecting out the top-$k$ spurious-predictive directions
($k\in\{0,1,5,20\}$) leaves coverage flat on ResNet-50 and raises it by at most
$0.02$---still sub-target---as the probe falls toward chance. Forcing invariance does
not unlock coverage; group-conditional calibration already delivers it from a fully
recoverable representation.

\paragraph{Calibrating with Predicted Groups}
\label{sec:predgroup}

Mondrian builds a threshold per group, which invites the obvious objection: those
group labels are exactly what a deployment does not have. Because the spurious
attribute stays easy to recover from the deployed features, as the probe results below
show, it does not need them.

The label $y$ is never predicted; only the attribute is. A probe fitted on the training
split's features predicts $\hat a$, the stratum is $\hat g=2y+\hat a$, and both the
thresholds and the test assignment use $\hat g$. Coverage is always scored against the
\emph{true} group, so a mis-assignment counts against us. The attribute is
then needed on the training split alone---not on the calibration split, and never at
test. The requirement is weakened, not removed: $y$ is still needed to calibrate, as in any split-conformal method.

Table~\ref{tab:predgroup} sets this fully deployable version against the ground-truth
oracle. The median coverage gap per setting
is $0.001$--$0.012$, and $30$ of $34$ methods stay within $0.02$ of the oracle. The
penalty is asymmetric by construction: the minority stratum carries the largest
threshold, so a minority point misplaced into a majority stratum is judged too strictly
and under-covered, while the reverse error only wastes efficiency. What is less obvious
is that probe accuracy does not order the failures---the setting with the lowest
$\auroc$ has none, and one at $\auroc=0.999$ has one. What travels with the penalty is
the method's own group-dependence: the largest failure is ERM's, and the next is GroupDRO-LL's, whose minority
points sit closest to the decision boundary. Reporting the probe's $\auroc$ is therefore
necessary alongside any such claim, but it is not sufficient
(Appendix~\ref{app:supp}).

\paragraph{When inferring the attribute is not acceptable.} This version predicts a protected attribute for individuals, which needs saying carefully. CelebA's
\texttt{Male} field is a binary annotation applied by third parties to photographs, not
self-identified gender; the stratum it induces should be read as ``the annotation that
co-varies with hair colour in this dataset'', nothing more. And inferring such an
attribute at test time is a different act from conditioning on one a person has
disclosed: it assigns a sensitive label to someone who has not provided it, wrongly for
$2$--$5\%$ of people even at the $\auroc$ we measure. We therefore scope the
recommendation. Predicted-group Mondrian is appropriate where the attribute is already
lawfully held, or where the inference stays inside an audit an operator runs on their
own system and never reaches a per-person decision. It is not appropriate where the
inferred label would be stored, disclosed, or used to route individual outcomes; there
the true-group version should be used, with attributes obtained by consent. Regimes
governing special-category data may prohibit the inference outright, whatever its
accuracy.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results compose into one story. Worst-group coverage is set chiefly by the
calibration rule: under one shared threshold it is at the mercy of the representation
and ERM collapses, while per-group thresholds make it nearly indifferent to training and
lift even ERM to match a robustly trained model. That survives retraining the
representation end-to-end. What training buys instead is efficiency, and only once
calibration is group-conditional. The rest follows: the apparent training-side reduction in cross-group divergence cannot be separated from an accuracy effect, being measurable at matched accuracy in one
setting out of eight; the most accurate method is the most efficient in six settings of eight;
coverage is stable under correlation shift and sub-target only in the min-over-groups
sense; and the representation does not hide the group, so coverage and auditing coexist.

One cost of that recommendation is worth stating, because it points the other way.
\citet{cresswell2024} give prediction sets to human decision-makers and find that
equalizing coverage produces the \emph{largest} disparity in outcomes of any rule they
test, precisely because it widens the gap in set sizes; they recommend equalizing set
sizes instead. Our data reproduces their mechanism. Moving from a shared threshold to
per-group thresholds raises the set-size disparity in all eight settings, from
$0.080$--$0.339$ to $0.168$--$0.608$ (Table~\ref{tab:pergroupcov}), and Figure~\ref{fig:overview}(d) prices it for a
single setting: the worst group's sets grow from $1.080$ to $1.319$ while the average
barely moves. Both results stand, and what separates them is which quantity carries the
guarantee. Where coverage is the thing being promised---an audit, a service-level
agreement, a regulatory floor---the calibration rule is the lever, and the cost is
larger sets for the group that was being under-served. Where a person reads the set and
acts on it, set size is what reaches the decision, and equalizing coverage buys
validity at a price measured in the wrong currency.

Three things follow for practice. Compare cross-group divergence \emph{at matched accuracy}, and treat an uncontrolled
comparison as uninformative rather than as a smaller version of the same effect: in the one setting
where matching is possible the controlled estimate is not smaller than the uncontrolled one, and in
the other seven no controlled estimate exists at all. A method chosen for worst-group accuracy is rarely a poor choice for the coverage shortfall. And take worst-group coverage from the calibration rule, which works
on ordinary, fully auditable representations and---because the group is
recoverable---needs no group labels at test time.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Our conclusions are empirical and scoped to two vision benchmarks, four frozen
backbones plus one fine-tuned end-to-end, and binary spurious attributes; a third dataset and a multi-class task would tell us whether the dissociation is specific to $|\mathcal{Y}|=2$, where a set can hold at most
two labels and the efficiency axis is correspondingly compressed---bounded above by two,
and, as Section~\ref{sec:c2} reports, not bounded below by one. Compressed in principle is not the
same as small in practice: the observed spread across training methods still runs $0.036$ to
$0.364$. The fine-tuning study
moves the representation for one architecture only, so its evidence is narrower than
the frozen grid's. The accuracy-matched comparison
is undefined when a method's accuracy support does not overlap ERM's,
which tells us something but prevents a uniform statement. Mondrian's
group-conditional coverage is, by construction, close to a per-group target, so
the non-trivial content is the \emph{magnitude} of marginal
calibration's representation-dependent failure and the size of the ERM lift, which
we quantify. Deployable Mondrian (Section~\ref{sec:predgroup})
holds for APS/RAPS and robust methods but degrades for the most group-dependent
model and the THR score, scoping that claim to robust models and rank-based scores;
it also infers a protected attribute at test time, so it should be used only where
such inference is permissible and auditable. Confidence intervals do not model
cross-dataset variance.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Worst-group conformal coverage is a calibration property far more than a
representation one, and the difference is large enough to change practice: giving the
worst group its own threshold lifts an ordinary model by up to $0.345$, more than any
training method we tried reaches under a shared threshold. Robust training still earns
its place---it buys smaller sets---but it should not be credited with reliability that
calibration is providing. We release the full grid, the representation study, the shift
sweep and the matched-accuracy protocol, so that training-side conformal claims can be
checked without the accuracy confound.

\subsection*{Proposition~\ref{prop:dissociation}}

\begin{proof}
(i) is the standard finite-sample split-conformal guarantee applied within each
group under within-group exchangeability. For (ii), the group-$g$ coverage is
$F_g(\hat q)$ by definition of $C$. Writing the pooled CDF as the mixture
$F=\pi F_{\wg}+(1-\pi)F_{\neg\wg}$ and using $F(\hat q)=1-\alpha$,
\[
1-\alpha=\pi F_{\wg}(\hat q)+(1-\pi)F_{\neg\wg}(\hat q)
        =F_{\wg}(\hat q)+(1-\pi)\big[F_{\neg\wg}(\hat q)-F_{\wg}(\hat q)\big],
\]
which rearranges to \eqref{eq:shortfall}. Inequality \eqref{eq:ksbound} follows since
$F_{\neg\wg}(\hat q)-F_{\wg}(\hat q)\le\sup_t|F_{\wg}(t)-F_{\neg\wg}(t)|=D_{\mathrm{KS}}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Which group is worst}

Proposition~\ref{prop:dissociation}(ii) is stated for a designated group and holds for
any group, so nothing that rests on it requires the worst group to be the same one
across models. Where the minimum lands is still worth knowing, because the answer
differs sharply between the two calibration rules.

Table~\ref{tab:argmin} reports where the minimum lands, and one column of it corrects an
assumption that is easy to carry into this setting. Under one shared threshold the ERM baseline's
worst group is stable---the same group in $85$--$100\%$ of runs---but it is not the same
\emph{kind} of group in the two datasets. On Waterbirds it is waterbird-on-land, which holds
$2.5\%$ of the calibration set: the small-$\pi$ reading of \eqref{eq:shortfall} applies, and the
group is a minority in the sense the spurious-correlation literature means. On CelebA it is a
group holding $47.5\%$. Compositing to $\rhocal=0.95$ gives the two label-attribute-aligned cells
$47.5\%$ each, so blond-male is a minority in the \emph{class} but not in the group prior, and the
shortfall there is not a small-$\pi$ effect. We say so because the mechanism paragraph in
Section~\ref{sec:setup} would otherwise read as covering both datasets when it covers one.

Under per-group thresholds the concentration drops sharply: no group holds the minimum in more than
$41\%$ of runs, against $25\%$ for a four-way tie. That is the direction validity in every group
implies, though not a demonstration of a tie---$30$--$41\%$ is above chance. What the numbers
support is the weaker statement that the minimum stops being pinned to one group once each group is
calibrated to its own level, so the identity of the worst group becomes largely
uninformative---and the minimum of four noisy coverages then sits below their mean by the amount
computed in the next subsection, which is the effect Appendix~\ref{app:subtarget} uses to explain
the sub-target level. The two columns ask different questions of different populations: the first
is ERM alone under one rule, the second every kept method under the other.

Two things follow. Divergence-based reasoning about coverage is applied run by run to
whichever group is worst in that run, never by assuming a fixed worst group across
models. And the identity of the worst group carries little information under
per-group calibration, which is why Section~\ref{sec:c1} reports the level of
worst-group coverage rather than which group attains it.

\subsection*{The minimum over groups, and where $1.03$ comes from}

Appendix~\ref{app:subtarget} uses the approximation
$\mathbb{E}[\min_g\cov_g]\approx(1-\alpha)-c_{|\mathcal{G}|}\,\sigma$. The constant is
the expected value of the minimum of $|\mathcal{G}|$ standard normals. Writing
$Z_{(1)}=\min_{j\le k}Z_j$ for i.i.d.\ $Z_j\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
c_k &= -\,\mathbb{E}\big[Z_{(1)}\big] \\
    &= k\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} z\,\phi(z)\,\big[1-\Phi(z)\big]^{k-1}\,dz ,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:minconst}
\end{equation}
using $f_{Z_{(1)}}(z)=k\phi(z)[1-\Phi(z)]^{k-1}$. Evaluating gives $c_2=0.564$,
$c_3=0.846$, $c_4=1.029$ and $c_5=1.163$. With four groups the reported minimum
therefore sits about one per-group standard deviation below the mean over groups even
when every group is exactly valid, which is the first of the two effects in
Appendix~\ref{app:subtarget}. Treating the per-group coverages as independent and
Gaussian is an approximation---they share a test set, and coverage is an average of
Bernoulli draws---so we use \eqref{eq:minconst} only to set the scale, and rely on the
simulation of Table~\ref{tab:subtarget} for the value we compare against.

\subsection*{Why $\Wone$ carries no bound}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:ksbound} bounds the shortfall by $(1-\pi)D_{\mathrm{KS}}$ because
$D_{\mathrm{KS}}$ is a supremum over thresholds and the shortfall is the CDF gap at one
threshold. No such bound holds for $\Wone$, and the reason is easy to see. Fix the
pooled threshold $\hat q$ and a target gap $\delta$, and let
$F_{\neg\wg}(t)-F_{\wg}(t)$ equal $\delta$ at $t=\hat q$ but fall to zero outside
$[\hat q-\varepsilon,\hat q+\varepsilon]$. Then
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Wone(P_{\wg},P_{\neg\wg})
&= \int\big|F_{\wg}(t)-F_{\neg\wg}(t)\big|\,dt \\
&\le 2\varepsilon\delta \;\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to0]{}\; 0 ,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:w1zero}
\end{equation}
while the shortfall stays at $(1-\pi)\delta$ throughout. So $\Wone$ can be driven
arbitrarily small at fixed shortfall: it integrates the discrepancy over the line, and
the shortfall depends on it at a single point. We report $\Wone$ in
Table~\ref{tab:div} because it is the summary used by \citet{burden2026}, and use
$D_{\mathrm{KS}}$ wherever a bound or a prediction is claimed.

\section{Why Mondrian Sits Below Target: The Min-over-Groups Effect}
\label{app:subtarget}

Mondrian's empirical worst-group coverage settles at $0.83$--$0.89$ rather than the
nominal $0.90$ (Section~\ref{sec:c1}). This appendix shows the level is what an
\emph{exactly valid} procedure produces at our calibration sizes, so it is a property
of the reported statistic and not a validity failure.

Two effects combine, both finite-sample. First, per-group split conformal with the
finite-sample-corrected quantile
$\lceil(n_g+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$ covers at $\ge1-\alpha$ \emph{in expectation over
calibration draws}, but we report $\min_g\cov_g$, and the minimum of
$|\mathcal{G}|$ noisy coverages is biased low: for approximately Gaussian per-group
coverages with standard deviation $\sigma$, $\mathbb{E}[\min_g\cov_g]\approx
(1-\alpha)-c_{|\mathcal{G}|}\sigma$ with $c_4\approx1.03$. Second, Mondrian estimates each threshold from that group's \emph{own} calibration
sample, and the samples are very unequal. On Waterbirds the two minority groups hold $2.5\%$ of the
calibration set each, a $19\times$ imbalance in nominal count relative to the aligned
groups---$5$--$12\times$ once distinct images are counted, since the aligned $g_3$ is itself drawn
$4.2\times$ over. On
CelebA the imbalance runs the other way once distinct images are counted rather than composited
rows: the aligned group $g_3$ is blond-and-male, and its nominal $47.5\%$ share is $140$ distinct
images drawn about fifty times each, against $340$--$361$ for the two nominal minorities
(Table~\ref{tab:data}). The least precisely estimated threshold on CelebA is therefore the one
belonging to a group the composited counts make look the best supported. Either way some threshold is estimated from far less information than its nominal count
suggests. \citet{ding2023classcond} study this regime directly and propose
clustering classes to pool calibration data when per-class counts are small.

The group that attains the minimum is not the same under the two rules, and the
simulation is about the Mondrian one, and under that rule the minimum is not tied to a
particular kind of group: it falls on the aligned $g_3$ in all four Waterbirds settings and moves
between groups on CelebA, with no group holding it in more than $41\%$ of runs
(Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}). Figure~\ref{fig:subtarget} puts our own runs against that law
directly. Simulated at
our own per-group counts, an exactly valid procedure puts the minimum at $0.8695$ on
Waterbirds and $0.8847$ on CelebA; we observe $0.8660$ and $0.8800$, with $98\%$ and
$95\%$ of runs inside the simulated central range. Both are simulated at the nominal per-group counts rather than the distinct-image counts,
which understates the noise on exactly the groups the minimum tends to fall on; the residual we
report below is therefore an upper bound on the part the law leaves unexplained.
Table~\ref{tab:subtarget} repeats the
exercise with no model, no data and no representation: four groups, i.i.d.\ continuous scores, exact exchangeability, per-group
conformal thresholds, and only the realised per-group calibration counts varied.

\section{Pre-Specified Hypotheses}
\label{app:hyps}

Appendix~\ref{app:excl}, before running any of the experiments they concern. They were
not lodged in a public registry, so they are pre-specified rather than preregistered.

returned. Two came back split, and Table~\ref{tab:hypverdict} records what each returned rather than leaving the verdicts spread across Section~\ref{sec:results}. H\ref{hyp:c1} has two clauses: that Mondrian's worst-group coverage is near target for

argues for rests on the second clause and on the size of the spreads, not on Mondrian reaching
target, which Appendix~\ref{app:subtarget} explains it does not.

\begin{hypothesis}[Coverage is a calibration property]\label{hyp:c1}
Under Mondrian, worst-group coverage is near target for every training method
including ERM, while under marginal split it falls below target by an amount that
depends on the training method---so the calibration policy governs worst-group
coverage far more than the representation does.
\end{hypothesis}
\begin{hypothesis}[Training buys efficiency]\label{hyp:c2}
Holding Mondrian fixed, worst-group set size differs by training method and tracks
base accuracy. \emph{As tested}, we read this against \emph{worst-group} accuracy rather than base
accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:c2}), which is the quantity the training methods are chosen to move; the
substitution was made when the analysis was written and we note it rather than restate the
hypothesis.
\end{hypothesis}
\begin{hypothesis}[Transfer]\label{hyp:h1}
Robust training lowers the accuracy-matched divergence relative to ERM (CI of
\eqref{eq:matched} excludes $0$ on $\ge 2/3$ scores at $\rhocal$).
\end{hypothesis}
\begin{hypothesis}[Ranking inversion]\label{hyp:h2}
The training method best for worst-group accuracy differs from the one best for
the worst group's coverage shortfall, with a CI-separated $\covgap$ gap.
\end{hypothesis}
\begin{hypothesis}[Shift survival]\label{hyp:h3}
Any reduction or coverage level holds across the $\rhotest$ sweep.
\end{hypothesis}
\begin{hypothesis}[No auditability tension]\label{hyp:audit}
The spurious attribute remains recoverable from the representation on which
worst-group coverage is obtained, so coverage and post-hoc group auditing coexist.
\end{hypothesis}
All may return negative; we report whatever the data show.

\section{Excluded and Flagged Methods, and Their Influence}
\label{app:excl}

\paragraph{Where the floors come from.} We set the per-method worst-group accuracy
floors before running any of these experiments, so no result influenced them, but we did
not register them publicly. The floors are $0.30$ for ERM and $0.75$ for the robust
methods on CelebA, and $0.30$ and $0.40$--$0.45$ on Waterbirds.

balanced subsampling seed $4$ there ($0.730$). Twelve DFR runs on CelebA sit just below
a soft reference of $0.85$ ($0.808$--$0.847$) and are kept, flagged.

values carried with a dagger in Tables~\ref{tab:c1} and~\ref{tab:h1}, reported so that

\paragraph{AFR's collapse is not an untuned hyperparameter.} AFR reaches
$0.686$--$0.890$ worst-group accuracy on Waterbirds but $0.013$--$0.029$ on the three
ViT backbones of CelebA. The mechanism is a class-prior inversion: at the default
$\gamma=2$ the reweighting drives the head to predict the positive class for
$\approx\!86\%$ of examples where the true rate is $\approx\!15\%$, so a
\emph{majority} group becomes the worst group. It is monotone in $\gamma$, and
$\gamma=1$ improves on ERM. Because reporting a method at an untuned hyperparameter
would be unfair, we computed an \emph{oracle} upper bound---the best $\gamma$ chosen
with knowledge of the evaluation set, which no selection rule could beat: $0.327$,
$0.427$, $0.445$ and $0.477$ across the four CelebA backbones, all far below the
$0.75$ floor. AFR's exclusion is therefore a property of the method in this regime,
not of our choice of $\gamma$.

\paragraph{What changes if they are put back.} The exclusions are of two
kinds, and they behave differently, so we report them separately. Restoring the three
individual seeds---GroupDRO-LL seeds $1$ and $4$ and balanced-subsampling seed $4$, all
on CelebA/ResNet-50---changes nothing, and it is worth being clear why. All three sit in a setting
whose across-method spread is $0.003$, while the maximum of $0.024$ is attained on Waterbirds, so
no plausible restoration of three seeds in that one setting could have moved the maximum: the
result is arithmetic rather than evidence. What it does establish is local---the spread in their
own setting shifts by under $0.001$---and the informative number is the method-level one below.

Restoring the whole excluded methods is the larger perturbation, and we recomputed every
result with them included. The dissociation is still unaffected: under Mondrian the
across-training spread rises only to $0.028$ at worst, against $0.024$ with the methods
excluded, while the marginal spread grows because the degenerate methods are themselves
badly under-covered. One thing does change and we report it: the pre-specified C1
criterion---Mondrian within $0.02$ of target \emph{and} marginal significantly below
target for \emph{every} method---is met in exactly one of eight settings (CLIP/CelebA),
and that single setting \emph{flips} to failing when the excluded AFR method is included, since
AFR's marginal coverage there is $0.695$. The criterion is therefore not robust to the exclusion rule, and we rest the claim on
the size of the spreads rather than on a pass/fail flag. Including a degenerate method also produces a spurious matched-accuracy
signal for AFR on CelebA/ResNet-50, which is a further reason to report both readings
rather than either alone.

\section{Procedures, Hyperparameters, and Statistics}
\label{app:hyper}
\textbf{Features.} Four frozen backbones, extracted once and cached: an ERM-trained
ResNet-50 ($d{=}2048$), CLIP ViT-B/32 ($d{=}512$, image--text contrastive), DINOv2
ViT-B/14 ($d{=}768$, self-supervised) and a supervised ImageNet ViT-B/16 ($d{=}768$).
All last-layer training runs on these cached features.
\textbf{Heads.} ERM (cross-entropy); DFR (group-balanced last-layer retraining);
AFR (automatic feature reweighting); balanced subsampling; last-layer GroupDRO.
\textbf{Calibration.} marginal split (one global quantile); Mondrian (a separate
quantile per group $g$, with a fallback to the pooled quantile when a group's
calibration count $n_g<50$); shift-robust (worst-case threshold over a
total-variation ball of radius $\epsilon=0.1$ around the calibration distribution;
under APS and RAPS results are insensitive over $\epsilon\in[0.05,0.2]$, trading $1$--$5$ points of
over-coverage for stability); $1-\alpha=0.90$. \textbf{Scores.} THR, APS, RAPS
(with standard tail regularization). \textbf{Shift.}
$\rhocal=0.95$; $\rhotest\in\{0.95,0.9,0.8,0.7,0.6,0.5\}$ via reweighted/composited
evaluation distributions, with realized $\rho$ logged. \textbf{Seeds/splits.}
$\ge3$ training seeds $\times\ge10$ calibration splits; $95\%$ CIs by bootstrap;
training-seed standard deviation and calibration-split standard deviation reported
separately per aggregate. \textbf{Why matching at split level misleads.} Pooling each method's
$(\mathrm{acc},D)$ points across seeds \emph{and} calibration splits is the natural way
to get enough points to interpolate, but the accuracy axis it produces is mostly
evaluation-draw noise. ERM's per-seed base accuracy is identical to five decimals in
every setting, because its last-layer solver is deterministic, so all of its apparent
accuracy spread comes from which evaluation rows were drawn. Matching on that spread
compares ERM on unlucky draws against a robust method on lucky ones, and a bootstrap
over the pooled points treats nested rows as independent draws. We therefore match at
model level, with one accuracy per training seed.
\textbf{Accuracy matching.} accuracy is overall (base)
top-1 on the evaluation distribution; from the per-seed $(\mathrm{acc},D)$ clouds of
$m$ and ERM we take $a^\star$ in the overlapping accuracy range and read each
method's $D(a^\star)$ by linear interpolation of its cloud, bootstrapping over
seeds; $\Delta(a^\star)$ is \texttt{unmatched} when the supports are disjoint.
\textbf{Recoverability.} StandardScaler $\to$ logistic
regression, in-domain train$\to$test; we also project out the top-$k$ spurious-predictive directions, $k\in\{0,1,5,20\}$. \textbf{Predicted-group
Mondrian.} the recoverability probe predicts the stratum $2y+\hat a$; thresholds
and/or test assignment use $\hat a$; coverage is always scored on true groups.
\textbf{Calibration/test construction (exact).} For each of $10$ split seeds the
evaluation set is partitioned once into disjoint halves by a seeded permutation; the
first half is the calibration reservoir and the second the test reservoir, so no row is
ever in both. Each reservoir is then resampled to its target correlation
strength---$\rhocal=0.95$ for calibration, $\rhotest$ for test---by drawing \emph{with
replacement} within that reservoir's own group indices, which is what makes an extreme $\rho$
reachable from a finite pool, and both draws are truncated to a common size
$n=\min(|\mathrm{cal}|,|\mathrm{test}|)$ so calibration and test sizes never differ
across $\rho$. The realised $\rho$ is logged per draw. Randomised scores (APS, RAPS)
take their tie-breaking uniforms from separate seeded streams for calibration and test.

\textbf{Composited counts are not sample sizes.} The draw is with replacement, so a group's
composited count can exceed the distinct images behind it, and Table~\ref{tab:data}'s last column
gives both. The minority groups are essentially subsampled, at $1.0$--$1.1$ rows per distinct
image, so their counts mean what they say. The \emph{aligned} groups are the inflated ones: mildly
on Waterbirds, where $g_3$ draws $1{,}245$ rows from $294$ images, and severely on CelebA, where
$g_3$ is blond-and-male and the natural data supplies only $140$ images to the calibration half
against a target of $7{,}095$ rows---about fifty draws each. That group's threshold rests on $140$
distinct scores, and on CelebA it is also the group attaining the minimum under a shared threshold.

distribution, but the split-to-split variation of Table~\ref{tab:variance}---which resamples the
underlying partition---is the part of the uncertainty that carries this.
\textbf{Head hyperparameters.} Every head standardises features using train-split
statistics, then fits a multinomial logistic model with L-BFGS, $C=1$ and at most
$5000$ iterations. DFR averages $10$ group-balanced subsamples; balanced subsampling
draws one; AFR uses $\gamma=2$, whose sensitivity is examined in
Appendix~\ref{app:excl}; last-layer GroupDRO runs $2000$ full-batch steps at learning
rate $0.05$ with group-weight step $\eta_q=0.05$ and $\ell_2$ penalty $10^{-3}$.
Fine-tuning (Section~\ref{sec:repr}) starts from the torchvision
\texttt{IMAGENET1K\_V2} ResNet-50 checkpoint and uses Adam at
$10^{-3}$ for ERM and reweighting, SGD at $10^{-3}$ with weight decay $10^{-2}$ for
GroupDRO, batch size $128$, mixed precision, and the last epoch rather than a selected
one.
\textbf{Shift-robust procedure (exact).} Given the calibration scores and the observed
total-variation distance $\epsilon$ between the calibration and test true-label score
distributions, the threshold is the $\min(1,\,(1-\alpha)+\epsilon)$ empirical quantile of
the calibration scores---the smallest level that guarantees $1-\alpha$ coverage for any
reweighting inside the TV ball.
\textbf{Seeds.} Training seeds $\{0,1,2,3,4\}$ for the grid and the fine-tuning study,
$\{0,1,2\}$ for the calibration and predicted-group ablations; calibration split seeds
$0$--$9$ throughout; bootstrap seeds fixed at $0$. Note that ERM and AFR are
deterministic given the cached features, so their across-seed variance is exactly zero.
\textbf{Compute.} The full grid and both ablations re-calibrate cached head posteriors,
and feature extraction runs once per backbone. The four-backbone grid takes $200$
minutes, the calibration ablation $183$, and the predicted-group study $92$, all on a
single machine with no GPU retraining.
\textbf{One method does not reproduce bit-exactly.} Re-running the evaluation for
Table~\ref{tab:pergroupcov} reproduced the released records exactly for ERM, DFR, GroupDRO-LL and
balanced subsampling---every value identical---but not for AFR, which differed in $36$ of its $480$
runs, by at most $0.013$ in worst-group coverage, on ResNet-50 and CLIP over Waterbirds. AFR is the
only method here fitted in two stages: a first head's converged probabilities become the sample
weights of a second, so a last-bit difference in the first solve is amplified into the weights and
can move a few test points across the conformal threshold. ERM shares AFR's features and solver and
is exact, so the features are reproducible and the effect is specific to that second stage. No
number in this paper changes---the reported values are the released ones, and the per-setting means
agree to within $0.0006$---but a reader re-running AFR should expect the third decimal to move,
and occasionally the second. The re-run measured coverage only, so the set sizes reported for AFR
are the released ones.
