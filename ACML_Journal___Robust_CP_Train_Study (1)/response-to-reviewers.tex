\section*{Reviewer 1}

\rpoint{R1.1}{the target is $0.90$ but the empirical values are $0.84$--$0.89$}

\begin{reviewer}
``In Section 3 \ldots the authors state that $(1-\alpha=0.90)$ is the target throughout. However,
their empirical values are around $0.84$--$0.89$, which are below the stated $0.90$ target. The
authors should clearly explain the reason for this difference.''
\end{reviewer}

The reviewer is right that the paper reported a sub-target number without an adequate explanation.
The cause is that we report a \textbf{minimum over four noisy per-group coverages}, which is a
downward-biased statistic even when every group is individually valid. We now demonstrate this with
a direct measurement rather than an argument.

\where{Section~5.1 (end), Section~5.5, and Appendix~C in full (Table~C8 and Figure~C1).}

\begin{revised}
Mondrian settles a little under the nominal $0.90$: averaged over the kept training
methods it lands at $0.856$--$0.884$ across the eight settings. The mean over
groups, which is what group-conditional calibration targets, sits at $0.9024$; the minimum over
four noisy per-group coverages sits $0.018$--$0.045$ below that mean, which is where an exactly valid procedure puts it.
\end{revised}

Appendix~C additionally simulates an \emph{exactly valid} Mondrian procedure with no model and no
data, and a new figure puts our evaluations against that law directly:

\begin{revised}
Simulated at our own per-group counts, an exactly valid procedure puts the minimum at
$0.8702$ on Waterbirds and $0.8847$ on CelebA; we observe $0.8660$ and $0.8799$, with $96.5\%$ and
$95.1\%$ of evaluations inside the simulated central range.
\end{revised}

The two effects are worth separating. The closed-form $1.03\,\sigma$ figure covers only the
min-over-$k$ selection effect and, with $\sigma$ the per-group coverage standard deviation across
training seeds and calibration splits, averaged over methods and groups, predicts
$0.017$--$0.036$ against an observed
$0.018$--$0.045$, so on its own it accounts for the level's direction and most of its size but not
all of it. Simulating the full Mondrian law additionally captures the second effect --- each threshold is
estimated from that group's own small calibration sample --- and the residual is then
$0.004$--$0.005$ of coverage. We report that residual rather than claim an exact match.

\rpoint{R1.2}{the backbones are frozen, so why the title claim?}

\begin{reviewer}
``The authors freeze the neural network backbones \ldots Therefore, why do the authors claim in the
title \ldots? The authors should clarify this claim or provide additional experiments where the
representation itself is trained or fine-tuned.''
\end{reviewer}

We have done both. This was the most substantial change to the paper.

\where{New Section~5.2 with Table~3 and Figure~4 (experiment); the title, abstract, Section~5.1 heading,
discussion, conclusion and related work (narrowing); Section~3 (terminology).}

We fine-tune the backbone \textbf{end-to-end} under three objectives --- plain ERM, GroupDRO and
group reweighting --- with five fine-tuning seeds each on Waterbirds and three on CelebA, holding
the head fixed at plain ERM so the
representation is the only thing that moves. The section opens with a \textbf{manipulation check},
because a representation-level experiment answers nothing unless the intervention actually moved
the representation:

\begin{revised}
GroupDRO fine-tuning raises worst-group accuracy from $0.516$ to $0.738$ on Waterbirds
($+0.222$) and from $0.396$ to $0.470$ on CelebA ($+0.074$), the largest gain any of our
representation-level objectives produces. It is not the largest in the paper---DFR moves worst-group
accuracy further at the last layer---but it is a substantial change to the representation, which is
what this section needs. Reweighting did not help ($0.466$ and $0.364$, both a little below ERM).
We keep it: a representation that fails to improve is still a representation that changed.
\end{revised}

The dissociation survives. Under marginal calibration the three representations span $0.249$ of
worst-group coverage on Waterbirds; under Mondrian they sit within $0.004$ of one another.

\textbf{Title narrowed.} ``Worst-Group Conformal Reliability under Spurious Correlation Is
a Calibration Problem, \emph{Not} a Representation Problem'' has become ``Calibration
\emph{Beats} Robust Training \emph{for} Worst-Group Coverage''. The new title states a comparison
we test rather than asserting what coverage is, and the subtitle was dropped with it. We traced the
same absolute claim through the rest of the paper and softened it in fifteen further places, since
softening only the title would not have answered the objection.

We also corrected a terminological conflation the reviewer's question exposed: the submitted paper
used ``representation'' for the frozen backbone, which it never varied. The revision uses
\textbf{backbone} for the feature extractor and \textbf{head} for the last-layer classifier, and the
experiment that changes the representation itself is the fine-tuning study.

\rpoint{R1.3}{only two backbones; compare more clearly with related work}

\begin{reviewer}
``Only two backbones are used, which limits the generalizability of the results. In addition, the
experimental results should be compared more clearly with those of other related studies.''
\end{reviewer}

\where{Section~4 and every results table (backbones); new Table~1 and the paragraph before
Section~5 (literature comparison).}

\textbf{Backbones: two $\rightarrow$ four,} chosen to span two architecture families and three
pretraining regimes --- an ERM-trained ResNet-50 ($d=2048$), CLIP ViT-B/32 ($512$, image--text
contrastive), DINOv2 ViT-B/14 ($768$, self-supervised, never label-trained), and a supervised
ImageNet ViT-B/16 ($768$). Every results table now reports all eight backbone--dataset settings,
apart from Table~1, whose published comparisons exist only for ResNet-50, and Table~E10, which
lists the two excluded ERM settings. The
substantive finding is that the two added backbones behave like the two originally reported: the
Mondrian cross-training spread never exceeds $0.024$ in any setting at APS.

\textbf{Literature comparison.} The comparison is unflattering and we present it as such --- on Waterbirds our ResNet-50 runs sit $12$--$26$ points below published worst-group
accuracies, and the gap varies by method rather than being uniform --- and
we state the two protocol differences that explain it: our runs refit only the last layer on
features from a deliberately light recipe, where the published GroupDRO numbers come from training
the network end-to-end and the published DFR and AFR numbers retrain a last layer on a stronger
base model, and worst-group
accuracy is read on our pooled evaluation domain rather than the benchmark test split. DFR comes
closest ($0.851$ against $0.883$ on CelebA) and AFR furthest on CelebA, where it degenerates (Appendix~E); on
Waterbirds last-layer GroupDRO is furthest.

We also explain why this does not bear on the paper's claims: every result is a
\textbf{within-setting} contrast computed on the same posteriors, so a weaker backbone lowers all methods in a setting together and leaves the calibration contrast intact. The four backbones span a
$0.20$--$0.95$ range of worst-group accuracy and the dissociation holds on every one of them, in seven of the
eight settings; on ViT-B/16/CelebA the shared threshold leaves almost nothing to recover.

\rpoint{R1.4}{more independent training seeds}

\begin{reviewer}
``The authors should increase the number of independent training seeds to ensure the reproducibility
and stability of the reported results.''
\end{reviewer}

\where{Section~4, new paragraph; Table~2 (intervals over seeds and splits); Table~B7 (seed-versus-split variance); Appendix~F (seed sets).}

The main grid runs \textbf{five} training seeds, as does the fine-tuning study on Waterbirds,
while the fine-tuning study on CelebA runs three; the
calibration-policy ablation runs three, and the manuscript states each count where it applies
rather than one figure that would not hold everywhere. In raising the count we found something we must disclose, because presenting it as
stability would be misleading: \textbf{two of the five training methods are deterministic.} ERM and AFR fit with
L-BFGS, whose solver ignores the random seed, so their across-seed standard deviation is exactly
$0.000$ and their base accuracy is identical to five decimals in every setting.

\begin{revised}
Extra seeds therefore add replicates for DFR, balanced subsampling and GroupDRO-LL
(across-seed SD up to $0.027$ in worst-group accuracy) and none for ERM or AFR, whose SD is exactly
zero in every setting. This is also why every interval here, apart from the correlation intervals,
is a cluster bootstrap that resamples training seeds and then calibration splits within them: the
ten splits inside a seed share one trained model.
\end{revised}

\rpoint{R1.5}{add the requested reference}

\begin{reviewer}
``The authors must add the following recent study to the references: `New unfreezing strategy of
transfer learning in satellite imagery\ldots', Scientific African, 2024.''
\end{reviewer}

\where{Section~5.2, in the fine-tuning protocol.}

Added, and placed where it is substantively relevant rather than appended to a list. Because the
revision now fine-tunes backbones end-to-end, how much of a pretrained network to unfreeze is a

\begin{revised}
How much of a pretrained network to unfreeze changes what the resulting representation
encodes, and full fine-tuning can distort pretrained features relative to linear probing (Kumar et

moves most, which pulls it as far as each objective allows and gives calibration the hardest test
our setting can offer.
\end{revised}

\rpoint{R2.2}{Proposition~1(ii)}

\begin{reviewer}
``Wasserstein-1 or KS divergence alone generally does not imply a monotonic reduction in coverage at
the pooled threshold. Please state sufficient assumptions for such a relationship or present the
divergence--coverage link as an empirical hypothesis.''
\end{reviewer}

The reviewer is correct and the earlier claim did not follow. Proposition~1(ii) is now built on an
\textbf{exact identity} rather than an assertion about divergences:
\[
(1-\alpha) - F_{\mathrm{wg}}(\hat q)
= (1-\pi)\bigl[F_{\neg\mathrm{wg}}(\hat q) - F_{\mathrm{wg}}(\hat q)\bigr],
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
(1-\alpha) - F_{\mathrm{wg}}(\hat q) \le (1-\pi)\,D_{\mathrm{KS}} .
\]

\where{Section~3, Proposition~1(ii) and the remark that follows it.}

\begin{revised}
$W_1$ admits no such bound: the gap can concentrate on a vanishing interval, sending $W_1$
to zero while the shortfall stays fixed. We therefore use $W_1$ only as a descriptive summary, its
role in Gao et al.\ (2026). Neither divergence orders coverage across models without assuming the
same group is worst in each, and we do not assume it: the shortfall identity and the KS bound are
applied evaluation by evaluation to whichever group is in fact worst. The assumption is a good approximation for
the ERM baseline under a shared threshold, where one group is the worst in $85$--$100\%$ of
evaluations, and a poor one under per-group thresholds, where no group holds the minimum in more
than $41\%$ of evaluations and the most concentrated setting is closer to the $25\%$ of a four-way tie than to the
shared-threshold figure.
\end{revised}

\textbf{A correction we owe this comment.} An earlier draft of the remark asserted that the
required stochastic ordering ``holds here by construction''. Checking it against our own records
before writing this reply, it does not: the realised worst group moves with the training method in
six of the eight settings, and under per-group thresholds it is near a four-way tie. Nothing in the paper
needed the assumption --- the identity and the bound hold for whichever group is worst in a given
evaluation --- so the claim has been withdrawn rather than repaired, and Appendix~A now reports where the
worst group actually lands (Table~A1). This is the reviewer's point confirmed rather than answered
away: divergence alone does not order coverage, and we no longer suggest that it does here.

A second, related premise also failed a check and has been corrected. The paper described
the group that comes out worst as the minority group the spurious correlation disadvantages. That
holds on Waterbirds, where it carries $2.5\%$ of the calibration set. On CelebA it does not:
compositing to $\rho_{\mathrm{cal}}=0.95$ leaves that group holding $47.5\%$, so the shortfall there is not the
small-$\pi$ effect the identity's second factor describes. Section~3 now scopes the mechanism to
the dataset it fits, Appendix~A reports the calibration share alongside the group index, and the
bound in Figure~2 is computed from each setting's realised group prior. The
bound still holds in all eight settings.

Every remaining statement in the remark was verified numerically before being written.

\rpoint{R2.3}{the accuracy-matched analysis}

\begin{reviewer}
``The current interpolation uses relatively few independent training seeds, and several robust
models show no overlap in accuracy with ERM. More controlled overlap and independent runs would
strengthen the analysis; otherwise, limit the conclusion to matched settings and use a hierarchical
uncertainty analysis that respects repeated calibration splits within the same trained model.''
\end{reviewer}

This comment led to the largest correction in the revision, and the reviewer's suspicion was better
founded than we realised.

\where{Section~5.4 (rewritten), Table~5 (recomputed), new Figure~5, and Appendix~F.}

\textbf{What we found.} The submitted analysis pooled each method's (accuracy, divergence) points
across seeds \emph{and} calibration splits, although its text described per-seed pairs. Measured separately, ERM's per-seed base accuracy is
identical to five decimals in every setting --- its solver is deterministic --- while its across-split
range is $0.008$--$0.026$ on Waterbirds and $0.036$--$0.051$ on the CelebA settings it is retained in. So the entire accuracy axis ERM contributed was \emph{which evaluation rows
were drawn}, not \emph{which model was trained}. Matching on it compares ERM on unlucky draws
against a robust method on lucky ones. The accompanying bootstrap over pooled points also treated
nested rows as independent draws, which is exactly the hierarchical-uncertainty problem the reviewer
raises.

\textbf{What we did.} We recomputed at model level (one accuracy per training seed) with a two-stage
cluster bootstrap that resamples seeds and then splits within them. ERM's accuracy support is then a
single \textbf{point}, and overlapping support exists in \textbf{one of the eight settings}:
GroupDRO-LL on ResNet-50/Waterbirds, whose per-seed accuracies bracket ERM's $0.9399$. There the
reduction is real and \textbf{replicates} the earlier estimate ---
$\Delta_{\mathrm{APS}} = +0.084\,[+0.064, +0.101]$,
$\Delta_{\mathrm{RAPS}} = +0.084\,[+0.063, +0.101]$,
$\Delta_{\mathrm{THR}} = +0.150\,[+0.129, +0.168]$.

\textbf{What we withdraw.} The two other matched values in Table~4 of the submitted paper --- GroupDRO-LL on
CLIP/Waterbirds ($+0.100$) and balanced subsampling on ResNet-50/Waterbirds ($+0.059$) --- rested on
the split-level spread. At model level neither method's accuracy support reaches ERM's, and both are
now reported as unmatched.

Following the reviewer's instruction, we \textbf{limit H3 to that one matched setting} and draw no
accuracy-matched conclusion elsewhere. We also note that the reason the comparison is unavailable is
itself the more robust reading: on CelebA the robust methods raise base accuracy to $0.81$--$0.91$
against ERM's $0.68$--$0.70$, so no common operating point exists at all.

\rpoint{R2.4}{impact of the excluded runs, and preregistration status}

\begin{reviewer}
``The failed AFR runs on CelebA and the excluded GroupDRO seed are informative for comparing
training interventions. Please include them in a sensitivity analysis and clarify whether the
exclusion rules were preregistered or only pre-specified.''
\end{reviewer}

\where{Section~4 (pre-specification, beside the exclusion rules), Appendix~D (the six hypotheses), Appendix~E (sensitivity analysis and the AFR
diagnosis).}

\textbf{Status of the rules.} They were \textbf{pre-specified, not preregistered.} The per-method
accuracy floors and the six hypotheses were fixed before any run reported here, so they are
pre-specified with respect to every number in the paper; the version history of the released code
records when, should the editors wish to check it. They were never lodged in a public registry,
and we do not claim otherwise. We have also corrected the paper's own
heading, which read ``Pre-registered hypotheses'' and now reads ``Pre-specified hypotheses'' with
the distinction stated.

\textbf{Sensitivity analysis.} Every excluded run was evaluated and its records retained, so
this needed no re-run. The two kinds of exclusion are now reported separately, because they are
different questions. Of the three individual seeds, only GroupDRO-LL seed 1 lies in the three-seed
calibration comparison where per-group thresholds are evaluated, and restoring it moves its
setting's spread from $0.0034$ to $0.0036$, far below the maximum of $0.024$; the other two belong
to the five-seed grid, which uses one shared threshold. The
appendix reports that as arithmetic rather than as evidence. Restoring the whole excluded
methods is the real test, and the dissociation survives it: the Mondrian cross-training spread
rises only to $0.027$ against $0.024$. \textbf{One thing does change, and we report it against
ourselves:} the pre-specified C1 criterion --- Mondrian within $0.02$ of target \emph{and} marginal
significantly below target for \emph{every} method --- is met in exactly one of eight settings
(CLIP/CelebA), and that single setting \emph{flips} to failing when the excluded AFR method is
included, since AFR's per-group coverage there is $0.858$, short of the $0.88$ the criterion asks
for, while its marginal coverage of $0.695$ meets the other clause. The criterion is therefore not robust to
the exclusion rule, and we rest the claim on the size of the spreads rather than on a pass/fail
flag.

\textbf{AFR's collapse is not an untuned hyperparameter.} Since excluding a method on a
hyperparameter we chose would be unfair, we investigated. The mechanism is a class-prior inversion:
at the default $\gamma=2$ the reweighting drives the head to predict the positive class for
$\approx 86\%$ of examples where the true rate is $\approx 15\%$, so a \emph{majority} group becomes
the worst group. We then computed an \textbf{oracle upper bound} --- the best $\gamma$ in $\{0.5,1,2,4\}$ chosen
with knowledge of the evaluation set, which no selection rule over that grid could beat --- of
$0.327$, $0.427$, $0.445$ and $0.477$ across the four CelebA backbones, all far below the $0.75$
floor. AFR's exclusion is a
property of the method in this regime, not of our choice of $\gamma$.

\rpoint{R2.5}{shift and predicted-group deployment claims}

\begin{reviewer}
``The rho sweep mainly appears to change group mixture/correlation strength rather than the
within-group data distribution, so the robustness claim should be scoped accordingly \ldots For
predicted-group Mondrian, describe exactly how predicted attributes determine thresholds, what
supervision is required, how probe errors affect coverage, and discuss the privacy or ethical
implications of inferring the CelebA \emph{Male} attribute.''
\end{reviewer}

All five requests implemented.

\where{Section~5.5 throughout ($\rho$-sweep scoping; mechanics, supervision, probe error);
Section~5.5, paragraph ``Ethical scope'', and the Declarations (privacy).}

\textbf{$\rho$-sweep scoping.} The reviewer's reading is correct.

\begin{revised}
The $\rho$ sweep changes the \emph{group mixture} of the evaluation distribution \ldots while the
class-conditional feature distribution within each of the four groups is untouched. It is therefore
a group-prior shift, a special case of label-and-attribute shift, and \emph{not} a general covariate
shift \ldots Our stability claim is scoped accordingly.
\end{revised}

We also now state a property of the sweep that the submitted paper did not. On CelebA the raw data
associate blond hair with being female, so compositing to $\rho=0.95$ builds the reverse
association, and lowering $\rho_{\mathrm{test}}$ there moves the evaluation distribution toward the
raw data (Section~4).

\textbf{Mechanics.} The label is never predicted; only the attribute is. Calibration points are
binned by $\hat g = 2y + \hat a$, where $y$ is known, and each bin's
$\lceil(1-\alpha)(n+1)\rceil$-th smallest score becomes its threshold. At test the label is not
available, so a candidate label $c$ is admitted when its score is at most
$\hat q_{2c + \hat a}$, the threshold of the bin that label would imply: the test side uses the
predicted attribute and nothing else. Coverage is always tallied against the
\textbf{true} group, so a mis-assignment is penalised rather than hidden.

\textbf{Supervision required.} The attribute on the \emph{training} split only --- not on the
calibration split, never at test. The reduction is from ``attribute labels on calibration
\emph{and} test data'' to ``attribute labels on training data only'': a meaningful weakening of the
requirement, not its removal.

\textbf{Probe error.} It propagates asymmetrically: the minority stratum carries the \emph{largest}
threshold, so a minority point misplaced into a majority stratum is judged against a tighter
threshold and under-covered, while the reverse error only wastes efficiency. We checked whether
probe accuracy predicts the penalty and it does \textbf{not} --- the setting with the lowest AUROC
($0.945$) has no failures, while a setting at AUROC $0.999$ has one. What travels with the penalty is
the method's own group-dependence: the largest failure is ERM's and the next is
GroupDRO-LL's. The probe's AUROC is necessary alongside any such claim, but not sufficient.

\textbf{Privacy and ethics.} Written as a constraint on use rather than a disclaimer. Two points are
stated plainly: CelebA's \emph{Male} field is a third-party binary annotation and \textbf{not
self-identified gender}, so a study that treats it as gender has erred before any ethical question
arises; and inferring such an attribute at test time assigns a sensitive label to someone who has
not provided it, and for some of them the label it assigns is the wrong one, even at the AUROC we
measure. The recommendation
is scoped to settings where the attribute is already lawfully held, or where the inference stays
inside an audit an operator runs on their own system, and it is explicitly ruled out where the
inferred label would be stored, disclosed, or used to route individual outcomes. We note that
special-category data regimes may prohibit the inference outright regardless of accuracy.

\rpoint{R2.6}{moderate the claims; improve reproducibility}

\begin{reviewer}
``Claims such as `training buys efficiency' and `accuracy is a good proxy' should be presented as
empirical tendencies \ldots Please also provide the exact calibration/test construction, per-group
counts, training hyperparameters, the shift-robust procedure, random seeds, and a link to archival
code with reproducible commands.''
\end{reviewer}

\where{Sections~5.3, 5.4 and 5.5 (moderation); Appendix~F with Table~F11 (reproducibility).}

\textbf{Moderation.} Four statements are now withdrawn rather than softened, because measurement
contradicts them.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.35em]
\item The per-setting accuracy--set-size correlation, reported bare in the submitted version, is
\textbf{uninformative in all eight settings} once a bootstrap CI over those methods is
attached --- each rests on only three to five methods, and with three a bootstrap interval is
uninformative however narrow it looks. We now report a \emph{direction}, not an estimate.
\item ``The absolute efficiency range is narrow'' is \textbf{withdrawn}. The spread runs $0.022$ to
$0.259$, and an equivalence check leaves the spread above a $0.10$ margin in six of
eight settings.
\item ``Training buys efficiency, not coverage'' is now stated as \textbf{conditional on Mondrian}:
under marginal calibration training still moves coverage, so it is not a general claim.
\item ``Mondrian is $2$--$4\times$ more split-stable than marginal calibration'' is
\textbf{withdrawn}. It does not survive the revised measurement: across
the eight settings Mondrian is in fact the \emph{less} stable of the two in five of them, both CLIP
settings of the submitted version among them (Section~5.5, Table~B7).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{revised}
Mondrian's advantage over one shared threshold lies in the \emph{level} it attains, not
in its variance: marginal's split-to-split SD is $0.007$--$0.043$, comparable to Mondrian's, and in
five of the eight settings Mondrian is the less stable of the two.
\end{revised}

That last one surfaced only when stability was re-measured split by split for the extension R1.3
asked for. ``Accuracy is a good proxy for conformal burden'' is
likewise a tendency: point-estimate inversions occur in five of eight settings but clear their
cluster-bootstrap interval in only one, by $0.014$ of coverage.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} Appendix~F now gives the exact calibration/test construction --- disjoint
reservoirs from a seeded permutation, resampled within their own group indices, truncated to a
common size, realised $\rho$ logged per draw, separate uniform streams for the randomised scores ---
the per-group counts for both datasets, every head hyperparameter, the training recipe of the
ResNet-50 backbone, the fine-tuning optimisers and epoch budgets, the shift-robust threshold in
closed form, the seed sets, and the compute each stage needs. We
also report the per-group calibration counts, whose smallest values are $66$ on Waterbirds and
$373$ on CelebA, so every Mondrian threshold rests on at least that many scores.

\textbf{Archival code.} A DOI will be minted from a tagged release and inserted before publication;
the manuscript currently carries a visible placeholder rather than a provisional link.

\rpoint{R3.1}{the calibration-versus-representation claim is too strong; provide CIs or equivalence
tests}

\begin{reviewer}
``The claim that coverage is a calibration property rather than a representation property seems too
strong \ldots Mondrian also does not always reach the target coverage. The authors should narrow the
claim and provide stronger statistical tests, such as confidence intervals or equivalence tests.''
\end{reviewer}

We agree on all three counts.

\where{Title, Section~5.1 heading and fifteen further places (narrowing); Tables~2, 4
and 6 (CIs); Table~2 and Section~5.1 (equivalence test on coverage); Section~5.3 (equivalence
test on set size); Section~5.1 and Appendix~C (sub-target level).}

\textbf{Narrowed.} The title is now comparative, and the same softening was applied throughout
rather than to the title alone. The heading of what is now Section~5.1, for instance, has changed from ``Coverage Is
a Calibration Property, Not a Representation One'' to ``Calibration Rule versus Training Method''.

\textbf{Confidence intervals.} The estimates that carry the paper's claims now come with a
bootstrap interval: Tables~2 and 6 resample training seeds and then the calibration splits within
them, and Table~4's correlation interval resamples the methods the correlation is computed over. Tables~3, 5 and 7 remain point estimates and are labelled as such --- Table~3's across-seed spread is given separately in Appendix~B, Table~5 reports
accuracy \emph{supports} rather than a single estimate, and Table~7 reports a median and a maximum.
The important addition is Table~2, which carries the central claim and previously had neither
intervals nor a test: its ERM lift and its across-method spread are both reported with intervals, and every lift
interval excludes zero.

\textbf{Equivalence tests.} Two, on the two different claims. On \emph{coverage}, which is what this
comment is about: an equivalence check that asks whether the upper end of a one-sided bootstrap
interval for the across-method spread under per-group thresholds falls below a $0.05$ margin, which
we chose after seeing the spreads and state as post-hoc in Section~5.1. It is, in all eight settings --- the
training method stops mattering once calibration is group-conditional. On \emph{set size}, the
same check against a $0.10$ margin does \textbf{not} establish equivalence in six of eight
settings, which is why we withdrew the description of the efficiency range as narrow.

\textbf{Mondrian not reaching target.} Addressed as under R1.1: the mean over groups is $0.9024$
against a nominal $0.900$, so the sub-target \emph{minimum} is a min-over-groups selection effect.
We continue to report the minimum, because it is the operationally relevant number, but now always
alongside the mean so the two are not confused.

\rpoint{R3.2}{``training buys efficiency'' is specific to Mondrian; the correlation is weak in one
setting}

\begin{reviewer}
``The claim that training buys efficiency rather than coverage also seems specific to Mondrian
calibration \ldots Moreover, the accuracy--set size correlation is weak in one of the four settings.
The authors should state this claim as conditional on Mondrian calibration and provide statistical
significance or uncertainty for the reported correlations.''
\end{reviewer}

Both implemented, and the reviewer's concern proved larger than stated.

\where{Abstract, introduction, Section~5.3, the discussion and the conclusion (conditioning);
Table~4 (intervals).}

The claim is now \textbf{explicitly conditional on Mondrian}. And with bootstrap intervals over the
methods, the correlation is uninformative not in one of four settings but in \textbf{all eight}
--- including the $-0.97$ and $-1.00$ we previously highlighted, which at $n=4$ methods are not

interval says nothing however narrow it is. Table~4 now prints each interval, italicised where it
spans essentially $[-1, +1]$ or rests on three methods, and the text reports a direction rather
than an estimate.

\section*{Further corrections}

Two substantive changes to the submitted paper that no review asked for, listed here so that
neither is a silent alteration.

\textbf{The coverage gap was defined inconsistently in Section~3}, contradicting both the
implementation and the submitted paper's own appendix. It now reads
$(1-\alpha) - \min_g \mathrm{cov}_g$, matching both.

\textbf{The Mondrian set construction gave every candidate label the quantile of the point's own
group} $2y+a$. For the labels other than the true one that reads the test label, so the sets were
not computable at test time. It is now label-conditional, $g(c)=2c+a$. Worst-group \emph{coverage}
is unaffected --- the true label meets its own group's quantile either way, and every coverage
number in the paper is unchanged --- but every set size was recomputed, and two readings moved with
them: per-group thresholds raise the set-size disparity in six of eight settings, not in all eight
as the oracle sets implied, and in the setting of Figure~1 the average set size now rises with the
worst group's instead of staying put. The released code and records are the corrected ones.

\noindent\textcolor{rulegrey}{\rule{\textwidth}{1pt}}

We are grateful for the care the reviewers took. The revision corrects two analyses, withdraws four
claims, and reports one pre-specified criterion as failing in seven of eight settings. We believe
the paper's central finding is stronger for it, since it now rests on eight backbone--dataset
settings and on a study in which the representation itself is retrained, rather than on the
absolute dichotomy the reviewers rightly questioned.
